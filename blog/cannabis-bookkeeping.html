Cannabis Bookkeeping: What Your Dispensary Actually Needs

The ledger, the tax work, and the money that has to move correctly before any of it gets recorded. Who should do each part, and how they fit together.

At a glance

  • Cannabis bookkeeping covers sales, cash, inventory and cost of goods sold, vendor bills, excise and sales tax, payroll, and the month-end close
  • 280E makes cost of goods sold the most important line in a dispensary's books; adult-use cannabis is still subject to it as of 10/06/2026
  • Most dispensaries run a standard ledger like QuickBooks Online, fed by the POS, payroll, and payables
  • The ledger only records. Paying vendors right, recovering vendor credits, and collecting receivables have to happen first
  • We run that money work done for you and post it to QuickBooks; your bookkeeper and CPA keep the close and the taxes

Cannabis bookkeeping is ordinary bookkeeping with three extra weights on it: a federal tax rule that punishes sloppy cost accounting, a state tracking system that already knows what you bought, and a banking system that won't move your money the normal way. A dispensary that gets the books right files a defensible return and knows its real margin. A dispensary that gets them wrong overpays the IRS, overpays its vendors, and finds out a year late.

This guide covers what cannabis bookkeeping actually includes, what to look for in a dispensary bookkeeper, what accounting software you need, and the part most operators miss: the money work that has to happen correctly before anything gets recorded.

What cannabis bookkeeping covers

For a dispensary or a cannabis brand, the bookkeeping job is to record every dollar accurately and close each month on time so your CPA can file and you can see where the business stands. In practice that means:

  • Sales. Posting daily sales from your point-of-sale, split by payment type, with discounts, returns, and taxes recorded separately.
  • Cash. Reconciling the drawers, the safe, and deposits. Cannabis runs on more cash than almost any other retail business, so cash reconciliation is a daily discipline, not a monthly one.
  • Inventory and cost of goods sold. Tracking what you bought, what you sold, and what you wrote off, so that cost of goods sold is correct. In cannabis this line matters more than anywhere else (more on that below).
  • Vendor bills and payments. Recording every invoice, every payment, and every credit a vendor owes you.
  • Taxes. Cannabis excise, sales tax, and local taxes, filed on your state's schedule.
  • Payroll. Usually through a payroll provider that will serve cannabis businesses.
  • Month-end close. Bank reconciliations, accruals, and financial statements your CPA can file from.

Why dispensary accounting is different

280E makes cost of goods sold the most important line

Section 280E of the tax code denies ordinary business deductions to businesses trafficking in Schedule I or II substances. For a dispensary, that means rent, payroll, and marketing largely can't be deducted against federal income, while cost of goods sold still reduces taxable income. So every dollar that properly belongs in cost of goods sold, and isn't there, costs you real tax. Which costs can be capitalized into inventory is a decision for your CPA, but your books have to be clean enough to support it.

The rules are moving. On 04/23/2026, the Department of Justice moved marijuana under a qualifying state medical license into Schedule III, and Foley Hoag's analysis of that order says qualifying medical operators should be able to claim standard business deductions. Adult-use cannabis is still Schedule I and still subject to 280E. A broader rescheduling hearing ran at the DEA this summer and, as of 10/06/2026, a decision is still pending. Ask your CPA how the change applies to your licenses before you change anything.

Metrc already knows what you bought

Every package that arrives at your store was recorded in your state's tracking system on a transfer manifest. That is a gift for bookkeeping: there is an independent record of what each vendor actually shipped you. Most books never use it. Invoices get keyed in as billed, and nobody checks whether the quantities match what the state says was delivered. Our guide to reading a Metrc manifest walks through that check.

Banking and payments are restricted

Many payment, payroll, and bill-pay products used by ordinary small businesses exclude cannabis in their terms. Your ledger can be standard software. The money movement often can't. See QuickBooks for cannabis for where that line falls.

What a good dispensary bookkeeper does

A good cannabis bookkeeper records what happened, accurately and on time, in a way your CPA can defend. When you're hiring one, look for:

  • Cannabis clients today. Someone who already closes books for licensed operators knows the excise schedule, the cash problems, and the cost accounting that 280E demands.
  • A working relationship with a cannabis CPA. The bookkeeper keeps the ledger; the CPA makes the tax calls. They need to agree on the chart of accounts and the inventory method.
  • Comfort with your POS data. Daily sales should post from point-of-sale reports, not from a bank deposit guess.
  • A close calendar. Books closed by a fixed date each month, every month.

What a bookkeeper usually isn't set up to do is the money work: matching every vendor invoice against what arrived, chasing vendors for credits, and collecting from customers who pay late. Those jobs get bolted on, billed at a professional rate, and done halfway. We break that down in cannabis bookkeeper vs. back office, and the staffing math in the true cost of a cannabis bookkeeper.

Cannabis accounting software: what you actually need

You don't need an exotic system. Most dispensaries and brands run a standard general ledger, very often QuickBooks Online, fed by three sources:

  • Your point-of-sale for sales, discounts, returns, and taxes.
  • Your payroll provider for wages and payroll taxes.
  • Your payables and receivables for vendor bills, payments, credits, and what customers owe you.

The ledger records. It doesn't check a vendor's invoice against the Metrc manifest, it doesn't notice that a return was never credited, and it doesn't chase a customer who is 60 days late. Those happen upstream, and the ledger is only as accurate as what's fed into it.

The work that has to happen before anything gets recorded

Clean books start with money that moved correctly. For a dispensary that means three things done every week:

  • Paying vendors right. Each invoice checked against the Metrc manifest and the delivery before it's paid, so you never pay for product that didn't arrive or for an invoice you already paid. At one multi-location retailer, a $4,873 duplicate invoice was caught before it was paid twice.
  • Recovering vendor credits. Returns, expired product, and agreed co-marketing add up to credits your vendors owe you. Nobody sends them unprompted. Credit recovery is a monthly job.
  • Collecting what you're owed. For brands selling wholesale, invoices slide from net 30 to net 90 unless someone works them. That's ShelfCollect.

Frequently asked questions

Does a dispensary need a cannabis-specific bookkeeper?

Yes, in most cases. A bookkeeper with current cannabis clients understands cannabis excise taxes, heavy cash handling, and the cost-of-goods-sold discipline that Section 280E demands, and already works with cannabis CPAs. A generalist can learn it, but mistakes in cost accounting show up as extra federal tax.

What accounting software do dispensaries use?

Most dispensaries and cannabis brands use a standard general ledger, very often QuickBooks Online, fed by their point-of-sale for sales, a payroll provider for wages, and a payables and receivables system for vendor bills, payments, and credits. The ledger records transactions; it does not check vendor invoices against Metrc or recover vendor credits.

Does 280E still apply to dispensaries in 2026?

For adult-use cannabis, yes. On 04/23/2026 the Department of Justice moved marijuana under a qualifying state medical license to Schedule III, which takes those medical operators out of 280E, but adult-use cannabis remains Schedule I. A broader rescheduling decision was still pending as of 10/06/2026. Confirm how it applies to your licenses with your CPA.
