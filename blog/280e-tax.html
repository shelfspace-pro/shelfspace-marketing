280E Tax Guide

280E Tax: What the Tax Code Says and What Cannabis Businesses Can Still Deduct

IRS section 280E denies all federal deductions and credits to the cannabis businesses it covers, leaving only cost of goods sold to subtract, and medical and adult-use operators now sit in different places. Here's where the tax code stands, what you can still subtract, and how to keep numbers your CPA can defend.

A dispensary can lose money on its books and still owe a large federal income tax bill. That's 280E at work, and it's why two cannabis businesses with the same sales can face very different tax results depending on how well their cost records hold up.

This guide is for the owner or controller staring at that bill. It covers what the tax code says today, what changed for medical and adult-use cannabis, what you can still subtract, and what to bring your CPA.

What IRS section 280E is

Section 280E is the part of the federal tax code that denies deductions and credits to a business that traffics in Schedule I or II controlled substances. The statute says "no deduction or credit shall be allowed" for amounts paid in carrying on such a business, where the trafficking is prohibited by federal law or the law of any state where the business operates. Congress added it in 1982.

State licensing doesn't switch it off. The IRS says the rule applies to businesses that sell marijuana even in states that have legalized the sale. It also reaches ordinary costs that aren't illegal at all: an IRS Chief Counsel memo names salaries, rent and telephone as examples.

280E is still in effect, and medical and adult-use now differ

Section 280E is still law, and it still applies to adult-use cannabis. What changed is the schedule of some marijuana. A federal final order effective 04/28/2026 moved marijuana subject to a state medical license, plus FDA-approved drug products containing marijuana, to Schedule III. The same order says any other form of marijuana stays in Schedule I.

Schedule III ends 280E only for activity that leaves Schedule I

Schedule III ends 280E for medical activity under a state license, according to the government's own statements, and it does nothing yet for adult-use. Four open points matter before you change how you file.

  • The guidance isn't out. Treasury and the IRS said on 04/23/2026 that they plan to issue guidance. The priority guidance plan dated 09/29/2026 lists "Guidance under §280E" and gives no date. We found no published notice or regulation when we checked the IRS plan page on 11/04/2026.
  • The order isn't a tax ruling. It says nothing in the rule is a determination of federal tax liability and sends licensees to tax counsel.
  • The first year is announced, not issued. Treasury expects a transition rule applying the change to the full taxable year that includes the effective date. If the guidance matches, that would mean all of 2026 for the medical activity of a calendar-year business.
  • Earlier years are unresolved. The order encourages Treasury to consider retrospective relief for years a licensee held a state medical license. Encouragement isn't relief, and the Treasury release doesn't grant any.

Adult-use could still move. A DEA judge stayed the broader rescheduling case on 09/29/2026 and, by an order dated 10/22/2026, kept it stayed only until briefing closes, with replies due 11/05/2026. We found no final rule in the Federal Register on that proposal as of 11/04/2026, so plan on adult-use staying under 280E until one is published.

The order also creates an expedited DEA registration process for state medical licensees, and on 10/30/2026 the Justice Department announced the first registrations, saying "State licensure alone does not automatically grant federal registration." The tax effect stays open, since the Treasury release doesn't say how registration affects the result. Put that on your CPA's list.

How to read a 280E adjustment

Read a 280E adjustment as the add-back that takes disallowed expenses out when you go from book income to federal taxable income. The phrase doesn't appear in the statute, so ask your CPA what they mean by it. Your books still record rent, payroll and marketing as real expenses, because they are. Your federal return for a business under 280E can't subtract them.

An audit can change the same numbers. In the Harborside case, the Journal of Accountancy reports that the IRS disallowed most of a dispensary's deductions and reduced its cost of goods sold, and the Tax Court sided with the IRS on the main issues in 2018. So an audit can hit both lines.

Why dispensary tax is so high

Dispensary tax is so high because a business under 280E pays tax on gross profit, while other businesses pay on net profit. The National Taxpayer Advocate's example from 05/10/2022 uses $1,000,000 in revenue, $750,000 in cost of goods sold, $200,000 in other expenses and a 30 percent rate: $75,000 in tax under 280E against $15,000 without it. The AICPA has said that 280E can produce a federal tax bill larger than net profit.

What you can deduct under 280E: cost of goods sold

Under 280E you can still subtract cost of goods sold, and almost nothing else. Technically it isn't a deduction. The IRS says 280E doesn't stop you from reducing gross receipts by your properly calculated cost of goods sold. What counts depends on what you do, and the IRS position is that you use the inventory-costing rules under section 471 as they stood when 280E was enacted.

  • Dispensaries (resellers). Under regulation 1.471-3(b), inventory cost starts with the invoice price less trade or other discounts, plus transportation and other necessary charges to get possession of the goods.
  • Cultivators and manufacturers (producers). Regulation 1.471-11 brings in direct materials, direct labor and certain indirect production costs such as production rent, utilities and supervision.
  • What stays out. The same regulation excludes marketing, advertising, selling, interest and general administrative costs.

You can't use the broader capitalization rules to get around this. Section 263A(a)(2) says a cost that couldn't otherwise be taken into account isn't capitalized under it. The Harborside court applied that reading and kept the dispensary's purchasing, handling, security, warehousing and cost accounting out of its cost of goods sold, leaving the purchase price and freight.

Sales themselves are income, so you can't write them off. Your method matters too. The same IRS memo says an examiner can require a business to change to an inventory method unless it's properly using a non-inventory method. Section 471(c) lets certain small taxpayers use other methods, so ask your CPA whether that helps you.

Vendor credits belong in this conversation. Since the reseller rule starts from the invoice price less discounts, a credit that never gets recorded can overstate your cost of goods sold, and a payment that can't be matched to an invoice is hard to defend. Ask your CPA how each kind of credit should be treated.

Aggressive 280E positions carry real risk

Filing as if 280E doesn't apply is a position you have to be able to defend. In fact sheet FS-2024-32, the IRS says some taxpayers disregarded 280E on rationales that don't meet the reasonable basis standard, and that a Form 8275 disclosure lacking a reasonable basis gives no penalty protection. Have your CPA or tax counsel put any such position, including a refund claim for an earlier year, in writing before you file.

What to bring your CPA now

You can prepare for the guidance today, whichever way it lands, by making your records able to answer the questions it's likely to ask.

  • Split activity by license. Tag sales, purchases and inventory as medical or adult-use, by location. If you sell both from one store, keep each side tagged separately until the guidance says how to treat you.
  • Pick an allocation basis with your CPA. Treasury mentions apportioning expenses but hasn't named a method, so keep sales, payroll hours and square footage by activity so you can support whichever one applies.
  • Tie costs to paper. The IRS says purchases, sales and payroll generate the supporting documents your books rely on. Every line in cost of goods sold should lead back to an invoice, a manifest and a payment.
  • Record every vendor credit. Returns, discounts and adjustments should reach the books when they happen.
  • Ask about the transition. The AICPA asked Treasury on 10/22/2024 for clarity on accounting year changes, partnership basis and depreciation, so raise those for your entity.

Frequently asked questions

What is IRS section 280E?

It's the part of the federal tax code that denies deductions and credits to a business trafficking in Schedule I or II controlled substances. It applies to cannabis businesses selling Schedule I marijuana even when their state licensed them. Cost of goods sold can still be subtracted from gross receipts.

Is 280E still in effect?

Yes. The statute hasn't changed, and it still applies to adult-use cannabis, which remains in Schedule I. Marijuana under a state medical license moved to Schedule III, and Treasury and the IRS say that generally removes 280E for that activity, with formal guidance still to come. A broader rescheduling case is still open with no final rule.

Does Schedule III end 280E?

Only for activity that actually leaves Schedule I and II. Today that means marijuana under a state medical license and FDA-approved products, so adult-use stays under 280E. A business doing both should expect to apportion expenses, based on what Treasury has said so far.

What is a 280E adjustment?

The phrase isn't in the statute, so ask your CPA what they mean by it. Read it as the add-back that removes disallowed expenses when you go from book income to federal taxable income. Your books still show rent, payroll and marketing, and your federal return can't subtract them. An IRS audit can also reduce both deductions and cost of goods sold.

Can you write off dispensary sales or expenses?

Sales are income, so there's nothing to write off there. Expenses for adult-use activity, such as rent, budtender payroll and marketing, can't be deducted under 280E. What you can do is subtract properly calculated cost of goods sold, which for a dispensary is mainly what you paid for product plus freight, less discounts.

Why is dispensary tax so high?

A business under 280E pays federal tax on gross profit, while other businesses pay on net profit. All the operating costs between those two lines get taxed as if they were profit. A store with thin real margins can end up owing more than it earned.
