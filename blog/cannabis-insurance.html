Cannabis Insurance: The Coverage Dispensaries Need and How to Get It

Cannabis and dispensary insurance is a smaller market with more exclusions and more questions. Here's what you need, what your state may require, and what to have ready before you apply.

A landlord wants a certificate by Friday, your regulator wants proof of coverage, and the broker you've used for years says the carrier won't write cannabis. Cannabis insurance is available, but it's a smaller market with more exclusions and more questions than ordinary commercial coverage. The operators who get decent terms arrive with their paperwork already in order.

This guide covers the coverage a dispensary or other operator actually needs, why it's hard to buy, the state minimums to check first, and what insurers want to see.

Cannabis insurance is available, mostly outside the standard market

You can buy cannabis insurance, and most of it comes from excess and surplus lines carriers rather than standard admitted insurers. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners' 2023 white paper update says that while a few states have admitted carriers, most of the industry buys through the excess and surplus lines market.

That matters when something goes wrong. The same NAIC paper says those carriers are generally exempt from state regulation, so state regulators can't easily measure coverage or affordability in that market. Before you buy, ask your broker whether the carrier is admitted in your state, and whether your license rules accept it.

The coverage a dispensary and other operators actually need

Most cannabis operators need general liability, product liability, property and workers' compensation as a base, then more based on what they do. The NAIC's 2019 regulatory guide names those four as coverages that apply across every business segment, and it lists the lines below among those the industry needs most.

  • General and premises liability. Injuries and property damage tied to your site and operations. Landlords and regulators commonly ask to see it.
  • Product liability. Claims that a product hurt someone, including contamination and labeling problems. Retailers sell the same products manufacturers make, so don't assume a supplier's policy protects you. Ask about recall coverage while you're at it.
  • Property and equipment breakdown. The building, buildout, fixtures, safes and equipment. Ask how the policy values inventory and whether cannabis stock is covered at all, because a property policy may treat it differently from your fixtures.
  • Crop coverage. Growing plants for cultivators. Availability varies. The NAIC guide contrasted a California grower paid on a crop claim with a state where regulators had found no insurer willing to write crop coverage.
  • Crime. Employee dishonesty, money and securities, and counterfeit money. The NAIC guide says rating here is highly affected by the cash nature of the business, and that brokers rank theft as the most significant hazard.
  • Auto and cargo. Delivery and distribution vehicles, plus the product inside them. A personal auto policy is not the answer for a driver running a route.
  • Workers' compensation. Generally required once you have employees. It can be one of the easier lines to place, since the NAIC's 2023 update says some admitted carriers write it for cannabis businesses.
  • Directors and officers, employment practices, errors and omissions. Claims from investors, employees and customers of your services. Multi-owner companies and anyone with outside investors should price these.
  • Excess or umbrella, and surety bonds. Extra limits above your base policies, and any bond your license or a contract requires.

Cyber coverage deserves a question too, since a dispensary holds customer and medical data in its point-of-sale system. The right mix depends on your license type, so read your state's rule before you pick limits.

State cannabis insurance requirements vary and can be specific

Your state or city may already dictate the coverage, the limits and even the kind of carrier you must use. Start there, since a policy that doesn't meet the rule won't satisfy your regulator however good the price looks. A few examples show the range:

  • Washington. WAC 314-55-082 requires commercial general liability or umbrella limits of at least $1,000,000, from an insurer authorized to do business in Washington with a rating of A- Class VII or better, with the state named as an additional insured.
  • Massachusetts. 935 CMR 500.105(10) requires general liability and product liability of at least $1,000,000 per occurrence and $2,000,000 in aggregate each, with a deductible no higher than $5,000 per occurrence, and an operator that documents it can't get coverage may instead escrow at least $250,000.
  • California. 4 CCR 15308 requires a distributor to carry commercial general liability of at least $2,000,000 in aggregate and $1,000,000 per loss, and it allows a qualifying surplus line insurer.
  • Local rules. San Francisco's 2025 bulletin requires distribution and delivery permittees to show proof of insurance on each vehicle, and it sets at least $1,000,000 in general liability for its grant recipients, so check your city as well as your state.

Rules change, so read the current text for your license type and confirm it with your regulator. A state minimum is a floor for keeping your license, and it may be well short of what a serious claim would cost you.

Why cannabis insurance is hard to buy

Cannabis insurance is hard to buy because federal law, cash and limited data all make carriers cautious. The NAIC's regulatory guide says the main obstacle for admitted insurers is the "lawful purpose" requirement, since a contract for an illegal purpose isn't binding and cannabis remains federally illegal. Its 2023 update says that tension leaves uncertainty over how policy language will apply, and that insurers remain reluctant to enter the market.

Rescheduling hasn't fixed it. A federal order effective 04/28/2026 moved marijuana under a state medical license to Schedule III while other marijuana stayed in Schedule I, so an adult-use operator is in the same federal position as before, and a medical operator should ask the carrier how it reads the change instead of assuming. A DEA judge stayed the broader rescheduling case on 09/29/2026 and, by an order dated 10/22/2026, kept it stayed only until briefing closes, with replies due 11/05/2026; no final rule has been issued.

Cash makes it worse. The NAIC's 2023 update ties cash handling directly to higher theft risk and added liability. Coverage that looks fine can also carry a hole: California's Department of Insurance warns buyers that policies may contain cannabis or public policy exclusions, and tells them to ask whether their business would be covered for a cannabis-related loss.

What cannabis insurers ask for

Insurers want to know what you do, how you protect it and whether your numbers hold up. The NAIC's regulatory guide says rates can turn on the number and type of licenses, the depth of your cannabis experience, the owner's financial position, any prior bankruptcies, video surveillance, locked storage, flammable solvents, employee selection and training, and background checks. It also says premiums can be based on sales or payroll, so the insurer may ask for both. Have this ready:

  • Licenses and legal structure. Every state and local license, the entity chart, owners and their experience.
  • Clean financials. Current statements, tax returns, sales by location and payroll that tie to each other. Ask your CPA to review anything that doesn't.
  • Vendor records. Who supplies your products, their licenses, certificates of insurance where you have them, and lab results.
  • Payment history. How you pay vendors and how much cash you handle. Showing that bills leave by bank transfer or check instead of envelopes tells a real story about your cash exposure.
  • Security and procedures. Camera coverage, vault and storage, cash handling and transport, and staff training logs.
  • Loss history. Past claims and incidents, with what you changed afterward. New ventures may pay a surcharge, so a well-documented plan helps.

The fastest way to improve this file is to reduce the cash. Our guide to how dispensaries handle cash covers the operating side, and clean bookkeeping makes the numbers easy to defend.

How to buy cannabis insurance without getting burned

Use a licensed broker who places cannabis regularly, get the exclusions in writing and read the policy before you need it. California's Department of Insurance offers a useful checklist: it keeps a list of agents and brokers it doesn't endorse, and it tells buyers to verify a license and to ask about cannabis exclusions. Most of that advice travels to any state.

  • Start early. Don't leave it until the week a lease, license or renewal comes due, because a specialist market may take longer to quote.
  • Verify everyone. Check the broker's license with your state, and ask whether the carrier is admitted or surplus lines.
  • Compare exclusions as well as premiums. Look for cannabis, THC, vape, recall, inventory valuation and theft limits, and for claims-made terms versus occurrence.
  • Match the policy to the paper. Your lease, loan, license and vendor contracts may each name required limits or additional insureds. Line them up in one sheet.
  • Tell the truth on the application. A wrong answer about your operations or cash can come back when you file a claim.

Frequently asked questions

What insurance does a dispensary need?

Most dispensaries need general liability, product liability, property and workers' compensation as a base. Crime coverage matters because of cash, and delivery operations need commercial auto. Check your state, your city and your lease, since each may set its own limits.

Is insurance required for a cannabis license?

In some states, yes. Washington and Massachusetts set minimum coverage for licensees, California sets it for distributors, and cities can add their own rules. Read the current rule for your license type and confirm it with your regulator.

Why is cannabis insurance hard to get?

Federal illegality makes many carriers cautious, since an insurance contract needs a lawful purpose. Heavy cash handling and limited claims data add to the concern. As a result, most coverage comes from excess and surplus lines carriers, and policies may carry cannabis exclusions.

What do cannabis insurers ask for?

Expect questions on licenses, owner experience, financial position, sales and payroll, security, cash handling, training and past claims. Having clean financials, vendor records and payment history ready makes the process easier. A well-organized file also helps a broker present your business.
