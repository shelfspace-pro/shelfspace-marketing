Cannabis Business Loans: Who Lends and How to Get Funded Faster

Most lenders won't touch cannabis, but some will, and they want a clean file. Here's who lends, what it really costs, and how to raise cash from what your customers already owe you.

You have a license, a real business and a cash problem, and the usual places to borrow have already said no. Cannabis business loans do exist. They come from a narrower group of lenders, they usually cost more than ordinary business credit, and the fastest money you can raise may already be sitting in your own unpaid invoices.

This guide covers who lends, what the SBA's current rule says, how to read the true cost of each option, and what to have ready before you apply.

You can get a business loan for cannabis, but most lenders won't offer one

Yes, you can get a cannabis business loan. It will most likely come from a private lender, a cannabis real estate company, an equipment lessor, a state program, or a community bank or credit union with a cannabis program. Large national lenders and SBA-backed programs are generally not where this gets solved.

Cannabis credit costs more for a reason a lender can name: marijuana's federal status limits both who can lend and what a lender can do if the borrower fails. The NAIC's 2019 guide describes bankruptcy dismissals for cannabis businesses and calls availability uncertain, so a lender can't count on that route. Our guide to what happens when a dispensary closes owing money shows how messy that gets for a creditor. Lenders price that in, and they ask for strong collateral to cover it.

Banks can lend to cannabis companies, and some do

Banks and credit unions can lend to licensed cannabis companies, and a small group publicly offers it. No federal rule grants them safe passage. FinCEN's guidance FIN-2014-G001 tells institutions serving marijuana businesses to verify state licensing, monitor the account and file suspicious activity reports. That compliance work is one reason many stay out or move slowly.

Public lists are the best place to start. New York's Office of Cannabis Management publishes institutions that serve cannabis businesses and notes which ones lend, without endorsing any of them. Terms tend to be conservative: one Minnesota bank says it will finance up to 75% of a commercial building purchase for an approved cannabis business. Where those lists name a loan type, it's usually real estate, vehicles or equipment.

Don't plan around Congress. The SAFE Banking Act of 2026 (H.R.9471) was introduced in the House on 06/25/2026 and a same-named Senate bill, S.4942 on 06/24/2026, and an introduced bill changes nothing for your application today.

SBA loans remain closed to most cannabis businesses

A business that touches the plant can't get an SBA loan. The SBA's current loan procedures, SOP 50 10 8.1, effective 10/01/2026 list as ineligible any business that "grows, produces, processes, distributes, or sells marijuana or marijuana products, edibles, or derivatives, regardless of the amount of such activity."

Rescheduling hasn't changed that language. A federal order effective 04/28/2026 moved marijuana under a state medical license to Schedule III while other marijuana stayed in Schedule I, yet the SOP that took effect on 10/01/2026 still says the bar "applies to recreational use and medical use even if the business is legal under local or state law." A DEA judge stayed the broader rescheduling case on 09/29/2026 and, by an order dated 10/22/2026, kept it stayed only until briefing closes, with replies due 11/05/2026; no final rule has been issued. Watch for changes, and until the SBA rewrites its rule, treat this door as shut.

Ancillary businesses need a closer look. The same SOP, applying 13 CFR 120.110(h), reaches businesses that make, sell, service, distribute or promote products or services used in connection with illegal activity, unless that use is completely outside their intended market. Landlords are caught too: real estate pledged for an SBA loan may not knowingly be leased to a marijuana dispensary. Hemp businesses can qualify if the hemp meets the federal and state definitions and the lender gets the documentation.

Cannabis lenders fall into five groups

Cannabis-friendly lenders fall into five groups, and the right one depends on what you're financing and how big you are. Start from the use of the money, then look for a lender whose own website says it finances cannabis.

State cannabis loan and grant programs are mostly for social equity licensees

State money exists, and most of it goes to social equity licensees or specific license types. Check each program's page for its current window, because rounds open and close.

  • New Jersey. NJ LEAF lends to recreational cultivators, manufacturers and testing labs that have operated for at least one full calendar year and have gross revenue of $5 million or less.
  • Illinois. The state's commerce department reports $31.8 million in direct forgivable loans to 95 social equity businesses in Round III of its program.
  • Colorado. The Cannabis Business Loan Fund has offered loans of $50,000 to $150,000 through a mission-based lender to operating social equity licensees with at least 18 months of revenue, and its page last shows a 2023 application window, so confirm funds are available.
  • Connecticut. The Social Equity Council posts a loan application for low-interest loans to redevelop unused property for social equity applicants, municipalities and certain nonprofits.
  • Maryland. The Cannabis Business Assistance Fund currently offers a milestone-based grant to social equity conditional licensees.

Cannabis funding for startups mostly comes from owners and state programs

Pre-revenue cannabis startups have the fewest options, so expect to rely on owner capital, private backers or a state program. Most lenders want history: one funding company says its cannabis financing is for operating businesses with verifiable revenue.

Private money has its own rules, so clear it with the regulator before you take it. Washington's WAC 314-55-035 requires a licensee to disclose the source of funds and has the state board run financial and criminal background checks on financiers. California's 4 CCR 15004 treats a person providing a loan as a financial interest holder you must disclose, with banks excluded. Have your attorney read the rule in your state before any money moves.

Invoice factoring and sale-leasebacks turn assets into cash

Factoring sells what your customers owe you, and a sale-leaseback sells the building you already use. Neither adds a conventional loan payment, but each has a price you'll keep paying.

Invoice factoring. A brand sells its receivables at a discount and gets cash now. A sale of accounts falls under UCC 9-109, so expect a lien filing, and under each state's version of UCC 9-406 a store that receives proper notice must pay the buyer of the invoice to clear its debt. That means your buyers will likely find out. Factors also judge your stores' payment records, so slow accounts may be rejected or cost you more.

Sale-leaseback. You sell your property and rent it back on a long lease. Innovative Industrial Properties says its program focuses on well-capitalized companies that have been through state licensing, and NewLake buys industrial and retail property the same way. You get a large check and give up ownership, and the rent becomes a long-term fixed cost. Have your attorney check the lease, and check your state's rules too, since a landlord with a profit share can become a disclosable interest holder under California's rule.

Merchant cash advances look cheap until you annualize them

A merchant cash advance is usually the most expensive money on this page, because a factor rate hides how fast you repay. A factor rate looks small next to an interest rate, and the payments start almost immediately.

Here's a worked example, not customer data, annualized from the payments and term the way an estimated APR disclosure works: a $50,000 advance at a 1.35 factor means repaying $67,500, and paying $357 each business day for about nine months works out to roughly 84% APR, while repaying the same total in about six months pushes it to roughly 126%.

Ask for the APR in writing. New York Financial Services Law 803 requires an estimated APR, finance charge, total repayment and estimated term on sales-based financing, and section 802 exempts single transactions over $2.5 million. Outside a state with a rule like that, calculate it yourself.

Read the debit terms too. In a case against a cash advance company, the FTC alleged in 2020 that businesses got less funding than promised and had money withdrawn after the advance was repaid, and the defendants agreed in 2021 to surrender $9,837,000 for refunds. Check the net amount you'll receive, any confession of judgment, personal guaranty, and whether you can reduce debits when sales fall. Stacking a second advance on a first one is how a cash squeeze turns into a closure.

What a lender asks for before saying yes

Lenders want proof that you're licensed, solvent and able to repay, so the speed of your approval depends largely on your file. One real estate lender says a specialty lender would typically review property, zoning and licensing status, ownership and entity structure, cash flow, sponsor experience and liquidity, title, value and the repayment exit. Expect to show:

  • Licenses, ownership charts and the disclosure history your regulator holds
  • Financial statements and tax returns that tie to each other, including how you handle Section 280E, which our tax guide explains
  • An AR aging, and for stores an AP aging, with payment history by account
  • Bank statements and your clean monthly books
  • Your debt schedule, including every advance and vendor plan

The AR aging does two jobs. It tells a lender whether your revenue turns into cash. It also shows you how much you could collect before you borrow at all. A dollar collected from a store that already owes you carries no interest, no origination fee and no lien.

Frequently asked questions

Can you get a business loan for a cannabis company?

Yes. Private lenders, cannabis real estate companies, equipment lessors, state programs, and some banks and credit unions finance licensed cannabis businesses. Expect more collateral, more diligence and usually a higher price than ordinary business credit.

Can banks lend to cannabis companies?

Yes, and a small group of banks and credit unions publicly offers cannabis lending, mostly secured by real estate or equipment and often limited to certain states. They're expected to follow federal anti-money-laundering guidance on marijuana businesses, and that work is one reason many stay out. Ask directly whether a bank lends or only holds deposits, because those are different programs.

Is a cannabis business eligible for an SBA loan?

No, not if it grows, processes, distributes or sells marijuana. The SBA's current procedures say this applies to recreational and medical use even when the business is legal under state law. Ancillary businesses need a closer review of what they sell and to whom, and hemp businesses can qualify if the hemp meets the legal definitions.

How can a cannabis startup get funding?

Most lenders want operating history, so startups usually rely on owner capital, private backers or a state social equity program. Clear any private money against your state's disclosure and vetting rules before you accept it. Some community banks will talk to businesses still seeking a license, but a loan will generally depend on collateral.

How do I read the real cost of a merchant cash advance?

Turn the factor rate into an APR using the net cash you receive and the real payment schedule. A factor rate looks small, but daily repayment over a few months can produce a very high annual rate. New York requires an estimated APR on sales-based financing under its disclosure law, and you can calculate one yourself anywhere else.
