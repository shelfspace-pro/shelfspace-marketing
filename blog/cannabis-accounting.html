Cannabis Accounting: What's Different, Who to Hire and What Software Fits

Cannabis books carry a tax rule, a cash problem and a payments problem most accountants never see. Here's what to look for in a firm, which software fits, and how inventory costing works under 280E.

Cannabis accounting is ordinary accounting with a federal tax rule on top that changes which costs count, plus a banking system that makes moving money hard. Get it right and you have a return you can defend and a true read on your margin. Get it wrong and you may learn about it in an audit years later.

This guide is for the owner or finance lead deciding who should do what. It covers what's different about cannabis books, how to choose a firm, what software fits, and how inventory costing works under 280E. For the daily ledger work, read our guide to cannabis bookkeeping.

What makes dispensary accounting different

Dispensary accounting differs in five ways: a tax rule that disallows all deductions and credits, inventory costing that carries the tax return, heavy cash, restricted payment tools, and state tracking data that can test your books.

  • 280E. The statute denies deductions and credits to a business trafficking in Schedule I or II substances, so operating costs don't reduce your federal taxable income. Since a federal order effective 04/28/2026, marijuana under a state medical license sits in Schedule III and adult-use stays in Schedule I, which means a mixed operator may need to track each activity separately. A DEA judge stayed the broader rescheduling case on 09/29/2026 and, by an order dated 10/22/2026, kept it stayed only until briefing closes, with replies due 11/05/2026; no final rule has been issued. Our 280E tax guide has the details.
  • Inventory carries the return. The IRS confirms that cost of goods sold still reduces gross receipts, so it's the line that matters most and the one examiners test.
  • Cash. A business receiving more than $10,000 in cash in one or related transactions must file Form 8300 within 15 days, on top of the daily work of reconciling drawers, safes and deposits. See how dispensaries handle cash.
  • Payment tools. Some payment products exclude cannabis, as Stripe's restricted list does, so read each tool's terms and plan how those payments will reach the ledger.
  • State tracking. Your state's tracking system, such as Metrc, may hold its own record of what arrived, and your books should agree with it.

GAAP inventory costing under 280E takes two sets of numbers

GAAP inventory costing and tax inventory costing answer different questions, so a business under 280E needs both numbers and a reconciliation between them. Your financial statements follow accounting standards, and the AICPA's cannabis accounting workshop frames the subject as moving from Code section 471 to ASC 330. Your federal return follows the tax rules.

On the tax side, the IRS position is that a business under 280E computes cost of goods sold using the section 471 inventory-costing rules as they stood when 280E was enacted. Section 263A(a)(2) blocks capitalizing a cost that couldn't otherwise be taken into account. Here's how the reseller rule and the producer rule sort common costs for tax.

Product or raw material, less discountsInIn

Three details decide real money. First, which side you're on: in the Harborside case, the Journal of Accountancy reports that a dispensary was held to be a reseller because it didn't own the product through the entire production process, leaving its cost of goods sold at purchase price plus freight. Second, some indirect production costs follow your books: regulation 1.471-11 ties items such as production-related depreciation and insurance to their treatment in your financial reports when that treatment is consistent with GAAP, so your GAAP choices can reach your return. Third, method: the IRS memo says an examiner can require a change to an inventory method unless you're properly using a non-inventory method.

Medical activity adds a question. If guidance removes 280E for it, as Treasury and the IRS have signaled, a mixed operator may be costing the same kind of inventory under different rules by activity. Ask your CPA now how the books would need to split.

How to choose a cannabis accounting firm

Choose a cannabis accounting firm that already files returns for licensed operators like you, can explain its 280E position in plain words, and will tell you what it won't do. Start by knowing which role you're hiring.

RoleOwnsWhen you need it
BookkeeperDaily entries, reconciliations, month-end close supportFrom your first day of sales
Cannabis CPATax return, 280E position, inventory method, audit defenseEvery operator, before the first return
ControllerClose calendar, cost accounting, internal controlsMultiple stores or entities, or a production operation
Fractional CFOCash forecast, margins, lender and buyer reportingWhen decisions outgrow the monthly statements

Ask every candidate the same questions, and listen for specifics.

  • How many licensed cannabis clients do you serve, and do they look like us (retail, cultivation, manufacturing, vertical)?
  • Have you been through an IRS exam on 280E, and what got adjusted?
  • Are we a reseller or a producer for tax, and what goes into our cost of goods sold?
  • How are you handling medical and adult-use activity while the guidance is pending?
  • Who actually does the work each month, and what date will the books close?
  • Will you sign off on our chart of accounts and inventory method in writing?

Be careful with anyone who promises 280E will simply go away. In fact sheet FS-2024-32, the IRS warns that some positions disregarding 280E lack a reasonable basis and that a Form 8275 disclosure won't protect them from penalties. You want an advisor who can name the risk of a position as well as its upside.

Cannabis accounting software options

Start with a standard general ledger, fed by the point of sale, payroll and payables, and look at an ERP if you run many entities or a vertical operation. The ledger isn't the hard part. The hard part is getting clean, source-backed data into it.

If a demo can't show that, the system won't answer an examiner's question either.

A cannabis chart of accounts that supports 280E

A cannabis chart of accounts should separate what the tax rules treat differently, so your CPA isn't rebuilding the year from memos. Have your CPA approve it, then build these splits in.

  • Revenue by location and license, with medical and adult-use apart.
  • Cost of goods sold by purchases, freight in, trade discounts, vendor credits and, for producers, direct labor and each indirect production cost.
  • Operating expenses by function and location, kept fine enough to support an allocation between activities.
  • Taxes collected and owed, with excise and sales tax in their own liability accounts.
  • Cash in transit, with separate accounts for drawers, safes and courier pickups.
  • Vendor credits receivable, so a credit you're owed is visible until it's applied.

Separate the money-moving work from the close

Separate the work that happens before anything gets recorded from the close: chasing invoices, cutting checks, tracking credits and following up on late stores. A skilled accountant keying payment data isn't doing the analysis you hired them for. Put accounts payable and accounts receivable on your list when you plan the team.

Frequently asked questions

What is different about cannabis accounting?

Federal 280E rules disallow all deductions and credits for Schedule I or II trafficking, so cost of goods sold matters far more than in other retail. Add heavy cash, payment tools that may exclude cannabis, and state tracking data that can test your books. Medical and adult-use activity may also need separate tracking now.

Do I need a cannabis CPA or a regular CPA?

Use a CPA with current cannabis clients. Cost of goods sold under 280E, the reseller versus producer question and the unfinished guidance on medical activity are specialized, and mistakes tend to surface in an audit. Ask whether they've been through an IRS exam on 280E.

Can a cannabis business use QuickBooks?

Yes. The ledger itself can work well for a store, small group or brand. Before you rely on a bank feed or payment add-on, read its terms for cannabis limits and plan how payments will reach the books.

When do I need a fractional CFO for cannabis?

When decisions outgrow the monthly statements, such as a new store, a loan, a cash crunch or a sale. A fractional CFO adds forecasting and reporting, and needs clean cost and cash data to work with. If your books lag or your cost of goods sold is unsettled, fix that first.

How is inventory costed under 280E?

The IRS position is that a business under 280E uses the inventory costing rules as they stood when 280E was enacted. For a dispensary, that's mainly invoice price less discounts plus freight. Producers can also include direct labor and certain indirect production costs.

Do you replace my accountant?

No. We aren't a CPA firm, and your accountant keeps the close and the tax work. We run AP, AR, payments and vendor credits and sync them to your books, so your accountant starts from clean, source-backed records.
