Credit Recovery

Recovering Vendor Credits on Cannabis Returns That Never Come Back

A customer brings back a defective pen. You refund them and tell them to keep it — regulators recommended that path. The compliance is clean. The credit is invisible. Here's how we find it anyway.

At a glance

  • In many cannabis markets, regulators advise retailers to let customers keep defective product rather than take it back into inventory
  • Physically returning product means re-intake in Metrc, a new package tag, quarantine, and a witnessed waste event — high labor, high audit risk
  • Letting the customer keep it avoids the compliance burden but leaves no Metrc trail, so generic credit recovery tools miss the return entirely
  • ShelfSpace mines POS and Metrc data for the signals these refunds leave behind — negative sales, defect-coded discounts, cluster complaints on a single batch, POS returns without a Metrc adjustment
  • Every signal traces back to a vendor and a package ID, so we can build a credit memo with full supporting data
  • Net effect: a comprehensive view of defective-product returns that most operators never see — and thousands more in vendor credits recovered per year

There's a scene that plays out in every dispensary in North America. A customer walks in holding a pen that won't hit, or a half-eaten edible that tasted like soap, or a pre-roll that tunneled. The budtender takes it back, refunds the sale, and tells the customer to take the defective item home and dispose of it. Nobody touches the product again. The compliance team breathes a sigh of relief. The register balances. The customer leaves happy.

And a vendor credit that your store is owed just became invisible.

This is one of the most common blind spots in cannabis credit recovery — and it's not because anyone is doing anything wrong. It's because the easiest, most compliant way to handle defective product in cannabis also happens to be the way that leaves zero trail for a generic AP tool to follow. If your credit recovery process depends on Metrc transfers back to the vendor, you're missing most of your return credits. We built ShelfSpace knowing that, so we go looking for them a different way.

Why Regulators Prefer You Don't Take It Back

On the surface, it looks backwards. Product came from a vendor, it's defective, you return it to the vendor, the vendor credits you. Simple. But in cannabis, that chain involves the state government. Every package is tagged. Every transfer is manifested. Every waste event is logged. And the moment defective product re-enters your inventory, it's back on your books as a state-tracked asset that has to be disposed of by the book.

In more than a few markets, regulators have quietly — and sometimes explicitly — recommended that retailers skip that path for low-value defective product. The reasoning is practical: they'd rather you refund the customer, document the defect, and move on than watch five different retailers mishandle a waste event and put their license at risk. The alternative is a compliance process with real teeth.

Path A — Physical Return

What it takes

  1. Budtender receives defective product from customer
  2. Compliance officer re-intakes into Metrc
  3. Generate a new package tag for the returned unit
  4. Move product to a locked, segregated quarantine area
  5. Schedule a witnessed waste event — often with a second employee present
  6. Log and destroy the product per state protocol
  7. File transfer manifest back to vendor (if required)
Time per event20–45 minutes
Path B — Customer Keeps It

What it takes

  1. Budtender logs the defect on the transaction ticket
  2. POS processes the refund or return
  3. Customer disposes of the product at home
Time per event3–5 minutes

...but the Metrc transfer-back trail that most credit recovery tools depend on? Never created.

Faced with that trade-off, almost every operator chooses Path B for anything under a certain value. It's faster, it keeps the customer happy, it avoids a compliance near-miss, and it's what the regulators themselves often prefer. The problem is that the dominant credit recovery playbook — the one most AP software and managed services use — only knows how to find returns that took Path A.

The Invisible Credit Problem

A typical credit recovery workflow works like this: pull Metrc transfer history, find transfers of product back to vendors, cross-reference against credit memos on file, flag every return without a credit. That approach catches Path A returns cleanly — we wrote an entire case study about a $1,847 credit we recovered exactly that way.

But for Path B, there is no transfer. The product never moved. Metrc shows the sale, then nothing. The POS shows the refund, then nothing. The defect lives in a budtender's note, a discount code, or the customer's memory. And the vendor — whose product caused the refund — never gets asked for a credit, because from the data's perspective the refund looks identical to a customer returning a tincture they just didn't like the taste of.

Multiply that across a year. A dispensary doing three million in revenue typically sees a defect rate between half a percent and one and a half percent. Do the math — that's $15,000 to $45,000 in defective product each year, the bulk of which is handled on Path B and never becomes a vendor credit. On a two-location operation, the number scales with it.

If your credit recovery only finds returns that physically shipped back to the vendor, you're catching the easy ones and walking past the majority.

How We Find Returns That Never Left the Store

Since the Metrc transfer signal isn't there, we reconstruct the return from the signals that are there. Every time a budtender processes a Path B refund, they leave a fingerprint — in the POS, in the Metrc sale record, in the transaction notes, in the discount pattern. Individually, any one of those signals could be noise. Together, they're a forensic trail.

Here's the process our analytics run every week on every store we manage.

1
Pull every refund and negative-value transaction from the POS
Refunds, voids of completed tickets, returns, and negative line items. We ignore voids of open tickets — those are just budtender corrections. What we want is the money that actually left the drawer.
2
Scan transaction notes and discount codes for defect language
"Defect," "broken," "DOA," "customer complaint," "goodwill," "manufacturer issue," "won't hit," "moldy," "tunneled." Every POS has a different vocabulary — we learn yours during onboarding.
3
Match each refund to the Metrc package and vendor of origin
A refund is just a negative sale — the package ID is in the transaction. We trace it through your Metrc package history to the inbound transfer, the manifest, and the vendor who sold it. That's who owes the credit.
4
Check for Metrc adjustments that never happened
If the POS shows a return but Metrc shows no corresponding inventory adjustment, that's the Path B fingerprint — the product was refunded but not put back on the shelf. High-confidence kept-return signal.
5
Cluster-analyze by batch and time window
Three refunds on the same Metrc batch inside seven days is a manufacturer defect, not three unlucky customers. Cluster patterns unlock the biggest credits — we surface them as a single line-item with full batch-level evidence.
6
Build a credit memo with the full evidence chain
Metrc package ID, original sale date and receipt, refund event, reason code, batch-level defect cluster if applicable, wholesale value. Vendors verify fast when the documentation is airtight. We send, track, and apply to your next settlement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do cannabis regulators tell dispensaries to let customers keep defective product?

Taking defective cannabis back into inventory is a compliance-heavy process. The retailer has to receive the product back in Metrc, often generate a new package tag, physically quarantine it in a segregated area, and then process a witnessed waste event before it can be destroyed. It's five to seven steps of labor and exposes the store to state audit risk if any step is skipped. In many markets, regulators tell retailers the easier path is to refund the customer, log the return in the POS, and instruct the customer to dispose of the product at home.

How do I get vendor credit on a defective cannabis product if I never shipped it back?

Most vendor agreements cover defective product regardless of whether it's physically returned — the defect itself is what triggers the credit, not the transfer back. The obstacle is documentation. You need the original Metrc package ID, the sale record, the refund event, and evidence that it was a defect (not a change of heart). ShelfSpace pulls this from your POS and Metrc, builds a credit memo with full supporting data, and sends it to the vendor.

How does ShelfSpace find refund credits when there is no Metrc transfer back to the vendor?

We run smart analytics across your full Metrc and POS data looking for the patterns that a kept-return leaves behind: negative sales transactions, voided completed tickets, defect-coded discounts, cluster refunds on a single batch within a short window, and POS returns without a corresponding Metrc inventory adjustment. Every match gets traced back to the originating package, the vendor, and the wholesale value. Then we build the credit memo.

Do vendors actually pay these credits?

Yes, when the documentation is clean. Vendors can verify the Metrc package ID, see the sale, see the defect pattern, and confirm the unit came from them. What they push back on is vague claims without data. ShelfSpace presents the full chain of evidence — package ID, sale timestamp, refund event, reason code — so the approval conversation is straightforward.
