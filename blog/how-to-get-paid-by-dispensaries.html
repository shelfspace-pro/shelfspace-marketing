Cannabis Collections Guide

How to Get Paid by Dispensaries: The Complete Guide for Cannabis Brands

You did the hard part. You grew it, made it, sold it and delivered it. This is the playbook for the last step: turning the invoice into money in your account, without losing the store.

At a glance

  • Most dispensaries that pay late aren't refusing to pay. They're short on cash, waiting on an answer about the invoice, or simply never asked.
  • Set terms in writing before the first order ships, and put any account more than 30 days past due on credit hold.
  • Send the invoice the day product ships, with a one-click way to pay, then follow up on a fixed schedule in your own name.
  • Give slow payers a payment plan, write down every promise to pay, and send a dated final notice around 60 days late.
  • Some states, like New York, have formal rules for unpaid wholesale invoices. Know yours before you need it.
  • Track two numbers every week: days sales outstanding and the share of your open balance that's past due.

If you sell cannabis wholesale, you already know the feeling. The product moved. The store reordered. And the invoice from six weeks ago is still open, sitting in a buyer's inbox under a pile of other vendors' emails. Learning how to get paid by dispensaries is as much a part of running a brand as growing or making the product, and most brands never get taught it.

This guide is the whole playbook for cannabis collections in one place: why stores pay late, what to set up before you ship, a day-by-day follow-up schedule, how to make paying you take one click, and what to do when an invoice just won't move. Every section links to a deeper guide if you want more.

For fresher numbers, see our own Fall 2026 dispensary payment data: 70% of open wholesale invoices on four brands' books were past due, and paid invoices took a median of 33 days.

The Cannabiz Credit Association's figure that more than 56% of cannabis invoices are past due was reported by MJBizDaily. Whitney Economics estimated $3.8B in delinquent payments in a report built on 2023 data (Ganjapreneur), and LeafLink wrote in October 2025 that delinquent payments passed $4B+ in 2024. Whatever the exact figure is today, it lands hardest on cultivators and brands, the people at the top of the supply chain who front the product.

Why dispensaries pay late

Before you can fix late payment, it helps to know what's actually behind it. In our experience, it's rarely one store deciding not to pay. It's usually one of five things:

  • Cash is tight. Dispensaries carry heavy taxes and thin margins, and many pay vendors in the order they get pushed. The brand that asks clearly and often gets paid first.
  • There's a question about the invoice. A price that doesn't match the order, a missing case, a damaged unit. A store with an open question will often just stop paying that invoice until someone answers it.
  • The invoice doesn't match Metrc. If quantities on your invoice don't match what was accepted on the Metrc manifest, the store's bookkeeper may hold the bill until it's reconciled.
  • Your reps don't want to ask. Sales reps own the relationship, and asking a buyer for money feels like it puts the next order at risk. So the ask doesn't happen, or happens once and stops.
  • There's no system. Reminders go out when someone remembers. Promises made on the phone live in one person's head. Nobody knows which store is 20 days late and which is 80.

Only the first of these is really about money. The other four are about process, which means they're fixable. For a deeper look at how net-30 quietly turns into net-90, read Cannabis Net Terms: How to Get Invoices Paid.

Set terms before the sale

The cheapest collection work happens before the first order ships. Three things to put in place:

  • Terms in writing. Net 15, net 30, COD: pick one per account and put it on every invoice. If the store's buyer and your rep have different ideas of what "net 30" means, you'll find out at day 45.
  • A credit hold rule. Decide now that any account with an invoice more than 30 days past due gets no new orders on terms until it's current, or ships cash on delivery. Then stick to it. A store that can keep ordering while it owes you has no reason to pay.
  • The right paperwork on file. Get the store's details, the name and email of whoever actually pays the bills, and their tax documents up front. See how to get a retailer's W-9 and resale certificate and collecting W-9s without the chase.

Then send the invoice the day product ships. Net-30 starts when the invoice lands, so every day you wait to send it is a day added to every receivable you have. Our guide to cannabis accounts receivable management covers the full setup.

A day-by-day follow-up schedule

Consistency beats intensity. One clear message on a known schedule gets paid faster than a flurry of angry emails followed by silence. Here's a schedule that works for most brands:

1

A few days before it's due: a friendly heads-up

The invoice amount, the due date and a pay link. No pressure, just a reminder that keeps you at the top of the pile.

2

On the due date: it's due today

Short and factual. Many stores pay on the day they're reminded, because the link is right there.

3

7 days late: first reminder

Name the invoice and the days past due. Ask whether there's a question about it, so disputes surface now instead of at day 60.

4

14 days late: a firmer note, and a call

Firmer tone, same one-click link. This is when a phone call to the person who pays the bills pays for itself.

5

30 days late: credit hold and a payment plan

New orders stop on terms. Offer a 0% payment plan so a store that's short on cash has a way to say yes.

6

60 days and beyond: final notice, then escalate

One dated letter with a firm pay-by date and the consequences you'll actually follow through on. If that passes, decide on next steps.

Every message should go out in your brand's name and include the same way to pay. Want examples of what brands actually send? Read how to collect on overdue cannabis invoices and the case study of 94 past-due reminders sent in one brand's name.

Write down every promise to pay

"We'll pay Friday" is the most common answer you'll get, and the easiest one to lose. It gets said on the phone or buried in an email, Friday comes and goes, and nobody notices for three weeks.

Every promise needs four things written down where your whole team can see it: which invoices, how much, by what date, and how they'll pay. Then two things should happen on their own. Reminders on those invoices pause, so you're not nagging a store that already said yes. And on the date, someone checks whether the money came in.

Send a final notice

By 60 days late with no answer, another reminder isn't a plan. What works is one clear, dated letter: what's owed, the date it has to be paid by, how to pay in a few minutes, and what you'll do if it isn't paid.

Know your state's rules

A few states have written rules about wholesale cannabis credit, and they can be the strongest tool you have. Two examples:

  • New York. The Office of Cannabis Management's delinquent payment guidance (revised October 2026) says retailers buying on credit must pay within 30 days, and suppliers report retailers who don't. A reported retailer can then only buy cash on delivery until the balance is cleared. Here's our step-by-step on New York cannabis delinquent payment reporting.
  • Rhode Island. Every sale needs a written contract or purchase order, and when it names no payment date, payment is due 30 calendar days after delivery. In 2023, MJBizDaily reported that regulators looked into a dispensary after cultivators said it owed them about $500,000.

Other states have proposed rules but not passed them: Massachusetts bills would cap credit at 60 days, and Michigan regulators floated denying licenses over unpaid-invoice judgments. See cannabis wholesale payment rules by state for where each state stands. Rules change, so check your regulator's current guidance before you rely on one, and talk to your attorney if you're unsure. Nothing in this guide is legal advice.

When to bring in an agency or an attorney

Most past-due invoices never need either. Consistent follow-up in your name, a one-click way to pay and a payment plan close the gap for most stores. One brand collected $417,560 in past-due invoices in its first 30 days on ShelfSpace, and a cannabis farm got 88% of its past-due balance paid in month one, all without an agency.

An agency or attorney makes sense when an invoice is far past due, the store has stopped answering entirely, and you've already decided you won't sell to them again. Know the trade-off going in: a collections agency typically keeps 25 to 50% of what it recovers and takes over the conversation with your customer. A lawsuit can win you the balance plus interest, but it's slow and it costs money up front. The two numbers to watch every week

You can't manage what you don't measure. Two numbers tell you whether your collections are getting better or worse:

  • Days sales outstanding (DSO). The average number of days stores take to pay you. The math: open receivables divided by credit sales for a period, times the days in that period. If you have $300,000 open and sold $450,000 on terms last quarter, your DSO is about 60 days.
  • Percent past due. The share of your open balance that's past its due date. If it's climbing, your follow-up isn't keeping up with your sales.

Every day you cut from DSO frees cash you already earned. For a hypothetical brand doing $14,000,000 a year wholesale, cutting 7 days frees about $270,000, once, for as long as stores keep paying on the faster schedule. That's payroll, a harvest or a supplier paid on time. For more on how slow receivables squeeze cash, read the AP/AR cash flow gap.

Frequently asked questions

How do cannabis brands get paid by dispensaries?

Cannabis brands get paid by dispensaries fastest when they agree on terms in writing before shipping, send the invoice the day product ships with a one-click pay link, follow up on a fixed schedule in their own name, offer a payment plan to stores that need more time, and send a dated final notice when an invoice is about 60 days late.

What should a brand do when a dispensary won't pay an invoice?

First find out why. Most late stores are short on cash or have a question about the invoice, not refusing to pay. Fix any dispute, call the person who pays the bills, get a specific date and payment method, and offer a payment plan if cash is the problem. If the invoice is still unpaid after a final notice, put the account on credit hold and decide whether to report it to the state, refer it to an agency, or talk to an attorney.

How late do dispensaries usually pay cannabis brands?

Late payment is common in cannabis wholesale. The Cannabiz Credit Association has reported that more than half of cannabis invoices nationwide are past due, and Whitney Economics estimated about $3.8 billion in delinquent payments across the industry in a report based on 2023 data.

Do any states require dispensaries to pay brands on time?

Some do. New York's Office of Cannabis Management requires retailers buying on credit to pay within 30 days, and suppliers report retailers who don't. A reported retailer can then only buy cash on delivery until the balance is cleared. Rhode Island makes payment due 30 calendar days after delivery when the contract names no date, and Washington allows no credit at all. Rules change, so check your state regulator's current guidance.

Should a cannabis brand use a collections agency?

Usually only as a last step. A collections agency typically keeps 25 to 50 percent of what it recovers and takes over the conversation with your customer. Most past-due cannabis invoices get paid with consistent follow-up in the brand's own name, a one-click way to pay, and a payment plan, which keeps the full amount and the store relationship.

What is DSO and why does it matter for cannabis brands?

DSO, or days sales outstanding, is the average number of days it takes stores to pay you. It equals your open receivables divided by your credit sales for a period, times the number of days in that period. Every day you cut frees cash you already earned, which matters more than profit for a brand with thin margins.
