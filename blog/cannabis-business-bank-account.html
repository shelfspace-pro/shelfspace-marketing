Cannabis Banking Guide

Cannabis Business Bank Account: Requirements, Documents and Fees

A cannabis bank account takes more paperwork, more time and more money than a normal business account. Here's exactly what to prepare, what it costs, and what to do if your account gets closed.

Maybe you're opening your first location and the bank down the street said no. Maybe a closure letter just arrived and your payroll runs next week. Either way, you need a cannabis business bank account, and the institution you need is going to ask for far more than an ordinary bank would. This guide lays out the requirements, the documents, the timeline, the fees and what to do if your dispensary bank account gets closed.

What you need to open a cannabis business bank account

To open a cannabis business bank account, you need an institution that accepts your license type, a complete document file and owners who can pass a background review. Each institution sets its own rules, but they all work from the same federal expectations.

FinCEN's guidance tells institutions to verify your state license, review your application documents, learn what you sell and who you sell to, and keep checking after the account opens. So every request you get traces back to one of those duties. Check these fit questions first, since they can end the conversation early:

  • License and activity. USF Credit Union limits its program to licensed Tier II and Tier III businesses in Florida, so ask which activities each institution takes.
  • Location. Numerica serves Washington businesses and also accepts applicants who've applied for a license with the state board, while TASI Bank works only with licensed businesses in California.
  • Membership. A credit union also has membership rules, and Santa Cruz Community Credit Union requires a business to sit within its field of membership.
  • Other states. Jonestown Bank & Trust says that if you operate in one of its listed states, it can support your cannabis businesses nationwide, so ask how each institution treats your other locations.

The documents a bank asks for: a practical checklist

Banks ask for proof of your license, your legal entity, your owners and your expected cash activity. Pull this file together before you call anyone. It's built from what Vermont Federal Credit Union, First Enterprise Bank, Evergreen Credit Union and Herring Bank publish, and your institution may ask for less or more.

Ownership thresholds differ, so have every owner's details ready. Vermont Federal asks about each person who owns 10% or more plus anyone with significant management responsibility, while First Enterprise defines a beneficial owner as anyone holding 25% or more. Collect details for every owner anyway, since an institution can ask for more than its minimum.

How long a dispensary bank account takes to open

Plan on days to weeks from a complete application, and longer if your ownership is complicated. Safe Harbor, a financial technology company that works through partner institutions, publishes a range of a few days to a few weeks depending on complexity, and TASI Bank says approval generally takes about one week.

Getting a first response is usually quick. Twin City Bank and Sound Credit Union both say a team member will reply within two business days of your form, but a reply isn't an approval. Cogent Bank says its team works quickly to onboard businesses without giving a number.

What slows a file down is usually fixable:

  • A license still pending, or one location's license missing from the packet.
  • Owners or managers who live out of state, which is on FinCEN's red flag list, so explain the setup up front.
  • Outside money you can't trace to a legitimate source, also a listed red flag.
  • Deposits, sales records and tax filings that tell different stories.
  • A vague entity name with no explanation of what the business does.

Apply to more than one institution at a time if you have a deadline, and tell each one what you're trying to accomplish and by when.

Cannabis bank account fee schedules

Published monthly fees start in the hundreds of dollars, and percentage-based programs, such as Santa Cruz Community Credit Union's, can reach thousands, often with an opening fee. Many institutions only quote after a conversation, so these published examples are a reference point and not a ranking:

  • Flat monthly fee, opening fee. Dedham Savings lists a $25 minimum deposit, a $250 one-time opening fee and a $400 monthly service fee.
  • Flat monthly fee by tier. First Enterprise Bank lists a $100 opening balance with a $400 monthly charge for growers, processors, dispensaries and similar businesses, and $200 for ancillary companies.
  • Percentage of incoming funds. Santa Cruz Community Credit Union's cannabis schedule, effective 02/01/2022, charges 0.4% in year one and 0.3% after that, with a $750 monthly minimum for licensed businesses.
  • Opening and annual review fees. That same schedule charges licensed businesses $1,000 to $2,500 to open and $850 to $1,700 a year for due diligence, based on monthly cash flow.
  • Item overage. Vermont Federal charges $15 when more than 200 checks process against the account in a statement period.

Other costs live outside the fee schedule. Armored cash pickup is often a separate contract, and Vermont Federal and Herring Bank both point to courier arrangements. Ask each institution for its full schedule in writing, then total a typical month, with your real deposit volume, your wires and your courier stops. Also ask how your deposits are insured, because one credit union cites the NCUA while another cites private share insurance from American Share Insurance.

What happens after the account opens

Opening the account starts an ongoing review, and falling behind on it can cost you the account. Santa Cruz Community Credit Union warns that missing annual due diligence deadlines may lead to additional fees or account closure, and Honor Credit Union says its banker collects monthly documents and visits on site.

Keep your account activity consistent with what you told the institution. FinCEN's red flags include cash deposits out of line with your reported tax figures or local competitors, third-party deposits, and mixing business money with personal or unrelated accounts. Cash transactions over $10,000 trigger a federal currency report, which is routine. Splitting cash up to avoid that report is a federal offense, so never do it, even if someone tells you it's a harmless way to keep things simple. What to do if your cannabis bank account is closed

Get the closure date in writing, secure your balance and move your payments before that date. Once those are safe, start replacing the account. You may never learn the full reason, so spend your time on the fix.

  1. Confirm the details. Ask for the closure date, how your remaining balance will reach you, and what happens to checks and transfers still in flight.
  2. List everything tied to the account. That means payroll, tax payments, rent, vendor ACH, merchant deposits and any recurring debits.
  3. Protect the next payments. Tell vendors and staff early if a payment date or method will change, and reconcile your books to the final statement.
  4. Apply elsewhere immediately. Use our directory by state and send your full file to more than one institution.
  5. Be upfront about the closure. Tell the new institution what happened and what you've fixed, since your history may reach it another way.
  6. Handle cash carefully in the gap. Keep records of everything, and don't move business cash through personal accounts or a friend's account.

Two rules explain why this can feel opaque. Federal regulations bar a bank from disclosing a suspicious activity report or anything that would reveal one exists, so a banker may not be able to give you a detailed answer. And when an institution ends a marijuana relationship, FinCEN's guidance calls for a "Marijuana Termination" report and urges it, where it qualifies, to use voluntary information sharing to alert a second institution of potential illegal activity.

A closure doesn't automatically mean you did something wrong, since programs themselves change. One bank's cannabis page now says it's no longer taking new accounts, and our guide to cannabis bank shutdowns covers keeping payments moving through a change.

Frequently asked questions

What are the requirements for a cannabis business bank account?

You need an institution that accepts your license type and location, an active or sometimes pending license, formation documents, a tax ID and identification for owners and signers. Expect questions about your expected cash and deposit volumes too. Each institution sets its own rules, so ask before you apply.

What documents does a bank ask a dispensary for?

Most ask for state and local licenses, formation papers, an operating agreement, your IRS EIN letter and ID for owners and signers. Many also want seed-to-sale records, point-of-sale reports, financial statements and security policies. Have all of it ready before your first call.

How long does it take to open a cannabis bank account?

Plan on a few days to a few weeks from a complete application, based on the published timelines we found. Complicated ownership, outside investors or missing licenses can take longer. Apply to more than one institution if you're working against a deadline.

How much does a cannabis bank account cost?

Published monthly fees start in the hundreds of dollars, and percentage-based programs can reach thousands. Some programs also charge an opening fee or an annual review fee. Armored pickup is often separate. Ask for the full fee schedule in writing and total a typical month before you compare.

What should I do if my cannabis bank account is closed?

Get the closure date in writing, confirm how your balance will be returned, and list every payment tied to the account. Move payroll, taxes and vendor payments first, then apply to several institutions with a complete file. Be upfront with the new institution about what happened.

Why won't my bank tell me why it closed my account?

It may not be able to. Federal rules bar a bank from revealing a suspicious activity report or anything that would show one exists, so its explanation can be limited. Focus on protecting your payments and opening a replacement account.
