How to Sue a Dispensary for an Unpaid Invoice, and What Gets You Paid First

A lawsuit can turn an unpaid invoice into a judgment. A judgment isn't money in your account. Here's the paper you need before you file, what suing really costs, and the cheaper steps that usually get the check written.

At a glance

  • Before you sue a dispensary, pull the paper: signed terms, the invoice, the Metrc manifest, proof of delivery, every message, and a dated demand letter.
  • Small claims court is cheap and fast but capped, and every state sets its own cap. Bigger balances go to regular civil court, usually with an attorney.
  • State court in a legal state is the natural home for a cannabis contract fight. Colorado's regulator has said in writing that contracts between licensees are enforceable.
  • The deadline to sue on a sale of goods is four years in New York and Michigan, and your own contract can shorten it.
  • Winning doesn't collect the money. A store that closes may leave nothing to collect.
  • Reminders, a payment plan and a final notice, sent in your name, settle many unpaid invoices long before a lawsuit makes sense.

The store took the product, sold it and reordered twice. The invoice from four months ago is still open. The buyer stopped answering in the summer, and your last two emails went unread. At some point every brand asks the same thing: can I just sue them?

You can. A dispensary is a business like any other customer, and an unpaid invoice is a broken contract. But a lawsuit is the most expensive way to collect, and the slowest. This guide walks through what you need before you sue a dispensary for an unpaid invoice, which court fits your balance, what it really costs, and the steps that usually get you paid before you ever file.

Not legal advice. Talk to a licensed attorney in your state before you file. Court figures below were checked on official court and statute pages as of 11/16/2026.

What you need before you sue a dispensary

A judge decides on paper, so build the file before you file the case. You need proof the store agreed to pay, proof of what it received, proof you asked for the money, and a dated final demand. A complete file also does work before any filing, because the store's owner can see how strong your case is.

  • The terms the store agreed to. A signed credit application, a purchase order or an email accepting your terms. It names the legal entity you're suing and the payment terms you're enforcing. A signed personal guaranty gives you a person to pursue if the company runs dry.
  • The invoice. Invoice number, date, amount, due date and terms, exactly as sent.
  • The Metrc manifest. The manifest shows the transfer was made and accepted in the state's own system. If your invoice and the manifest disagree, fix that first. It's the store's easiest defense.
  • Proof of delivery. A signed delivery receipt or the accepted manifest, plus any credit you issued for shorts or damage.
  • Every message. Reminders, replies, promises to pay and any dispute the store raised. A written promise to pay confirms the balance in the store's own words.
  • A demand letter. One dated letter with the balance, a pay-by date and what you'll do next. Our guide to the final notice letter for an overdue invoice covers what goes in it.

Then total it. A one-page statement listing each invoice, its date, amount and days past due, tied to its manifest, is the first thing an attorney or a small claims judge will ask for.

Small claims or a regular civil suit

Small claims court is built for exactly this: a business owed a modest sum, a short hearing, low fees and often no lawyers. The catch is the cap. Each state sets its own limit, and some set a lower one for businesses. Above the cap, you file a regular civil case, which takes longer and usually needs an attorney.

Here's how the four states we see most often compare, as of 11/16/2026:

  • California. The courts' small claims self-help page says you can generally sue for up to $12,500, or up to $6,250 if you're a business. You can ask a lawyer for advice before court, but you can't have one with you in court.
  • New York City. A corporation, partnership or association files a "commercial claim" in the city's Civil Court. Commercial claims are capped at the small claims limit under section 1801-A of the New York City Civil Court Act, and section 1801 sets that limit at $10,000. Section 1809-A limits a business to five commercial claims per calendar month. Other New York courts outside the city have their own limits, set by state law.
  • Michigan. The small claims division hears money claims up to $7,000, the cap in effect since 01/01/2024 under MCL 600.8401. Under MCL 600.8408, attorneys can't take part, and a business is represented by an officer or employee with direct, personal knowledge of the facts.
  • Colorado. The Judicial Branch's small claims information sheet (dated 10/12/2026) covers cases up to $7,500 and limits a filer to 2 small claims a month and no more than 18 a year per county. Its self-help page adds that you can't split a larger claim into smaller ones.

Run your balance against the cap before you pick. As an example, say a Colorado store owes you $9,000. Colorado's self-help page says you can file a small claims case for more than $7,500, but you can only collect $7,500 if you win. So you either give up $1,500 for a faster, cheaper case or file a regular civil case for the full amount. That's a business decision, and a good one to make with your attorney.

The real cost and time of suing a dispensary

Filing fees are the small part. The real costs are your time, an attorney's time in a regular civil case, serving the store, and the months between filing and a ruling. Small claims trims all of that, but even there you'll prepare the file, attend mediation or a hearing, and then still have to collect.

Colorado's information sheet shows what the steps look like even at the small claims level. You must have the defendant served within 30 days of filing the case. After service, you take part in mediation in the next 60 days. Mediation is ordered in these cases, and each side pays the mediator $60 unless the court finds it indigent. If the store is a company, you need the name, phone number and email of someone there who can take part.

A regular civil case adds discovery, motions and attorney fees that can rival a modest invoice. Unless your contract or a statute lets you recover fees, you usually carry your own. Before you file, ask your attorney for an honest estimate, then compare it to what you'd net after collecting.

Whether a court will enforce a cannabis contract

In a legal state, a state court is the natural place to enforce a sale between two licensees, and some states say so outright. Colorado's cannabis regulator told licensees in writing that their purchase-and-sale contracts are enforceable and pointed them to the civil courts. Federal court is a different and riskier place for a cannabis contract.

Colorado's Marijuana Enforcement Division issued Industry Bulletin 23-01 on 03/09/2023 after reports of licensees not paying invoices for product they ordered and received. It says contracts between licensees for the purchase and sale of regulated marijuana are enforceable under section 13-22-601 of the Colorado Revised Statutes. It encourages licensees to consider their contract terms and civil court processes to recover unpaid amounts. It also says the division doesn't take part in contract disputes between licensees.

Federal court is where the plant's federal status gets in the way. Cannabis is still federally controlled, and courts have split on whether contracts involving it can be enforced, as a 2019 NAIC white paper describes. A collection case between two licensees in the same state usually belongs in state court anyway. If anyone suggests federal court, ask your attorney to explain the risk first. Other legal states have their own rules on cannabis contracts, so ask what yours says.

The statute of limitations clock on an unpaid invoice

An unpaid invoice is usually a contract for the sale of goods, and the Uniform Commercial Code gives you a fixed window to sue. In New York and Michigan, that window is four years from the breach, and your agreement can shorten it to as little as one year. Read your own terms before you assume you have time.

New York's UCC section 2-725 and Michigan's MCL 440.2725 use the same words: an action for breach of a contract for sale must be started within 4 years after the cause of action accrues. By the original agreement, the parties may reduce the period to not less than 1 year, but may not extend it. The claim accrues when the breach happens, whether or not you knew about it.

States adopt their own versions of the code and have their own general limitation rules, so the clock in California or Colorado may not match. The safe habit is simple. Treat the due date of the oldest unpaid invoice as the day the clock may have started, and talk to your attorney well before any deadline gets close.

Collecting a judgment from a store that may close

A judgment says the store owes you. It doesn't pay you. Colorado's court information sheet puts it plainly: if you win a judgment, you must collect what is owed, and the court doesn't do that for you. A store that is closing may leave nothing behind to collect.

Colorado's sheet also warns that a defendant may be "judgment proof," with no wealth, property or income that can be garnished. Even though you win, the judgment may be worth little to you. That's why the paper you signed before the first shipment matters so much. A personal guaranty lets you pursue an owner instead of an empty company, and your attorney can tell you what else your state allows.

If the store has already gone dark, the steps change. A court may have appointed a receiver, and a claim process may come with its own deadline. Read what to do when a dispensary closes owing you money before you spend anything on a suit. A slide toward closing usually shows up in your ledger first, and our guide to the warning signs a dispensary is about to stop paying covers what to watch for.

The cheaper steps that usually get paid first

Most stores that pay late aren't refusing to pay. They're short on cash, have a question about the invoice, or have nobody asking on a schedule. Steady reminders, a payment plan and one dated final notice resolve those cases for a fraction of what a lawsuit costs, and the store can keep buying from you.

Who should sue, and who should wait

Suing makes sense when the balance is large enough to justify the cost, the store has stopped answering, the paper is solid, and you've decided you won't sell to it again. If any of those is missing, the cheaper steps usually win. Run them first, and keep the lawsuit as the option the store knows you'll use.

Frequently asked questions

Can a cannabis brand sue a dispensary for an unpaid invoice?

Yes. An unpaid wholesale invoice is a broken contract, and state courts in legal states hear these cases. Colorado's cannabis regulator has said in writing that contracts between licensees are enforceable and pointed them to the civil courts. This isn't legal advice, so talk to a licensed attorney in your state before you file.

Should I use small claims court or a regular civil suit?

It depends mostly on the balance and your state's cap. Small claims is faster and cheaper, and often keeps lawyers out of the hearing, but every state limits how much you can recover there, and some set a lower limit for businesses. Larger balances go to a regular civil case, usually with an attorney.

How long do I have to sue a dispensary over an unpaid invoice?

For a sale of goods, New York and Michigan give four years from the breach under their versions of UCC 2-725, and the original agreement can shorten that to as little as one year. Other states have their own rules, so check your contract and ask your attorney early.

What should I send a dispensary before I sue?

Send a dated final notice or demand letter that lists each invoice, the total owed, a firm pay-by date, a simple way to pay, and what you'll do if it isn't paid. Before that, steady reminders and an offer of a payment plan settle many unpaid invoices without a lawsuit.
