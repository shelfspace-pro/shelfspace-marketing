Return and waste credits: the one vendor credit that applies to every order

Defective returns and destroyed product are a loss on a unit that was supposed to sell. It's the single credit type that works the same whether you bought the product wholesale or took it on consignment.

At a glance

  • Return and waste credits apply to both consignment and wholesale product — the only credit type that does
  • The math is simple: unit cost × quantity × your return policy rate (100% by default)
  • Only vendor-side returns count — defective, unsatisfied-customer, faulty hardware. Changed-mind returns stay with you
  • Every line is tied to a Metrc package tag, so the vendor can reconcile it against the regulator's ledger
  • Waste means product recorded as destroyed in Metrc because it expired or arrived defective

A vendor's cartridge comes back because the battery threading was stripped. An eighth of their flower expires on your shelf and you record it as waste in Metrc. In both cases you paid for a unit that was never going to make you a dollar, through no fault of your own. That is exactly what a return or waste credit recovers — and unlike every other vendor credit, it works the same way no matter how you brought the product in.

That last part matters more than it sounds. Co-marketing and inventory-aging credits only apply to wholesale product, because on consignment the vendor already shares every discount through their revenue split. Returns and waste are different. A destroyed unit isn't a discount — it's a total loss on inventory that was supposed to sell. So this is the one credit you can ask for on every vendor, on both paths, without any asterisk.

What actually qualifies

Two buckets, both backed by hard data:

  • Customer returns — vendor-side reasons only. Defective product, unsatisfied customer, faulty hardware. A customer who changed their mind or bought the wrong thing is your cost of doing retail, not the vendor's, so those reasons are filtered out before anything is charged.
  • Waste (destruction). Product you recorded in Metrc as destroyed because it expired on the shelf or arrived defective. Metrc is the regulator's ledger — if a unit was destroyed, there's a record; if there isn't, we don't claim it.

Each line carries the POS SKU, the Metrc item, and the Metrc package tag. The vendor's accounting team can pull their own Metrc report and reconcile every row, which is why these lines almost never get disputed. This is the same line-item backup you see walked through in the anatomy of a vendor credit memo.

The math

There's no keystone or margin calculation here — a return or waste credit is priced off what you paid, not what it would have sold for:

credit = unit cost × quantity × return policy rate

The return policy rate is the vendor's agreed share of the cost, which defaults to 100%. Most vendor agreements make defective returns and expirations fully the vendor's responsibility, so the default recovers the whole unit cost. If a particular vendor only stands behind, say, 50% of expirations, you set their rate to 50% and the platform bills half.

Worked example

Returns: 6 defective cartridges · unit cost $12.00 · 100% policy
  6 × $12.00 × 1.00 = $72.00
Waste: 10 expired eighths · unit cost $18.00 · 100% policy
  10 × $18.00 × 1.00 = $180.00
Vendor credit this month: $252.00

Cost is resolved per unit from what you actually paid — the invoice or receipt cost on file — so the credit reflects your real exposure, not a retail figure. Two hundred and fifty-two dollars from one vendor in one month, on product that was never going to sell, is money most stores simply write off because nobody itemizes it.

The guardrails

A few rules keep these credits clean and keep the vendor relationship intact:

  • Changed-mind returns are excluded. Only defensible, vendor-side reasons are charged. If a return doesn't have a reason that points at the product, it doesn't bill.
  • No double-counting. If a unit is both returned and then destroyed as waste, it's credited once, not twice — the platform dedupes a return that's subsumed by a destruction of the same package.
  • Consignment returns route separately. On a consignment order the return is netted through the weekly settlement at the vendor's contracted split, not billed as a wholesale credit, so a "both" vendor never gets charged twice for the same return.
  • Metrc or it didn't happen. No package tag, no claim. This is what makes the credit defensible instead of a negotiation.

One operational note that pays off here: Dutchie's "Return to Inventory = No" setting skips the Metrc record, and a return that never hits Metrc can't be credited. Standardize your team on "Return to inventory = Yes" so the destruction is recorded and the credit is recoverable.

What to do Monday morning

Three things, in order:

  • Confirm your return workflow records vendor-side returns and destructions in Metrc — not just in the POS. That's the source of truth the credit is built on.
  • Check that each vendor's return policy rate matches your actual agreement. The 100% default is right for most, but a vendor who only covers part of expirations should be set explicitly so you're never over-asking.
  • Run the month. The platform aggregates every qualifying return and waste line per vendor into one Metrc-backed credit memo you review and send — the same monthly cycle covered in generating a month of credit memos yourself.

Return and waste credits are the floor of credit recovery, not the ceiling. On wholesale vendors you'll layer co-marketing credits and inventory-aging credits on top. But this is the one to start with, because it's the one that applies to every vendor you have. Frequently asked questions

Do return and waste credits apply to consignment product?

Yes. Return and waste credits are the one credit type that applies to both consignment and wholesale product. On a consignment order the vendor already shares every discount through their revenue split, so co-marketing and aging markdowns do not apply — but a defective return or a destroyed unit is a straight loss on a unit that was supposed to sell, and the vendor covers their agreed share of the unit cost on either path. On consignment that share is netted through the weekly settlement rather than billed as a wholesale credit.

Which returns get charged back to the vendor?

Only vendor-side returns — defective product, unsatisfied-customer, faulty hardware. A customer who simply changed their mind is not the vendor's problem, so those reasons are excluded before anything is charged. Every charged return is tied to a Metrc package tag so the vendor can pull their own Metrc and reconcile the row.
