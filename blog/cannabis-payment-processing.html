Cannabis Payments Guide

Cannabis Payment Processing: What Works at the Register and Between Businesses

Credit cards are off the table for cannabis purchases, cashless ATMs carry real risk, and pay-by-bank has its own trade-offs. Payments between licensed businesses are a different story, and they work today.

You searched for a cannabis merchant account because cash is costing you time, or because a processor went quiet and your register stopped taking debit. Either way, you need a straight answer on what works, what's against the card rules, and what will hold up next quarter.

This guide covers dispensary payment processing at the checkout and the separate job of paying between licensed businesses. Those two get lumped together under "cannabis payment processing," and they have very different answers.

Cannabis payment processing is two separate problems

Cannabis payment processing splits into customer checkout, where a shopper pays a dispensary, and business-to-business payments, where one licensed business pays another. Card network rules dominate the first. The second can run on bank rails such as ACH and checks where a bank will process them, and it's the one you can move off cash today.

Dispensaries can't take credit cards for cannabis

Dispensaries can't accept credit cards for cannabis purchases under the card networks' rules, whatever a sales rep tells you. A federal watchdog report from August 2026 states that Mastercard and Visa prohibit the use of their cards to purchase cannabis, and that this is largely why accepting customer payments is hard.

Visa's own wording is general. The public Visa Rules edition dated 10/24/2026 never uses the word cannabis, but it says a transaction must be legal in both the cardholder's and the merchant's jurisdiction, and section 1.5.1.4 bars an acquirer from knowingly accepting a transaction that is illegal. Cannabis's federal status is what puts it in the path of that language.

Rescheduling hasn't changed the picture. A federal order effective 04/28/2026 moved marijuana under a state medical license to Schedule III, while adult-use stayed in Schedule I. A DEA judge stayed the broader rescheduling case on 09/29/2026 and, by an order dated 10/22/2026, kept it stayed only until briefing closes, with replies due 11/05/2026; no final rule has been issued. An organization representing the card networks told GAO the companies wouldn't change their policies unless cannabis is legalized at the federal level, and one processor selling to this industry says plainly that Schedule III does not make card acceptance compliant for plant-touching businesses.

So treat any "cannabis credit card processor" offer with suspicion. GAO relays an industry association's account of businesses using deception, such as recording the sale of a non-cannabis item and providing cannabis as a "gift." The same report relays a banking association's warning that a business caught breaking the policy may be banned, along with its owner, from future use of the company's cards. Mainstream processors are direct about this too: Stripe's restricted list names cannabis products and dispensaries as prohibited.

A cashless ATM is a debit purchase coded as a withdrawal

A cashless ATM is an in-store device that runs a shopper's debit card, codes the charge as an ATM withdrawal, and hands back the difference in cash. GAO describes the device as disguising the transaction so it appears to be a cash withdrawal, and says the amount may be rounded up to the nearest $20 denomination.

Card networks moved against it for different reasons: Visa because the transactions were miscoded, and Mastercard because cannabis sales are federally illegal. A Visa compliance memo dated 12/02/2021, as quoted by Marijuana Moment, said these devices "are used for purchase transactions, which are miscoded as ATM cash disbursements," and warned of penalties, without naming cannabis. In reporting from 07/26/2023, a Mastercard spokesman quoted in The Spokesman-Review said it had instructed financial institutions offering payment services to cannabis merchants to "terminate the activity."

In fairness, the option hasn't vanished: Flowhub's guide, updated in August 2026, still describes it under the name point of banking, with customers generally paying in fixed increments, and warns that some providers have disguised cannabis debit transactions. GAO reports that these transactions may violate Visa and Mastercard rules, and that representatives from four organizations said cashless ATMs and other electronic payment methods can stop working without warning. If your register depends on one, have a backup ready and read your contract with your attorney.

Pay-by-bank moves the money over ACH instead of card networks

Pay-by-bank is a checkout where the shopper links a checking account and the purchase is pulled over ACH, the bank network that also carries direct deposit and bill payments. It sits outside the card networks, which is why cannabis point-of-sale companies such as Dutchie and Flowhub offer it at the register.

  • Dutchie. Its Pay by Bank page says transactions batch at the end of each day and settle the next business day, with payment at the POS, a kiosk, an app or online.
  • Flowhub. Its payment guide says pay-by-bank funds typically settle within 1 to 3 business days, and that fees vary by provider.
  • Aeropay. Its cannabis page describes acceptance by QR code, payment link, text or email with no new hardware.
  • CanPay. A company release describes consumers paying cannabis retailers through a debit to their checking account over the ACH network.

Those descriptions are the companies' own, so verify them against your contract. Pay-by-bank has real friction as well: a cannabis industry representative told GAO that ACH can take customers several steps to connect, and some are reluctant to do it. A financial industry representative in the same report said institutions may be unwilling to process online payments, including ACH, for cannabis businesses. Ask your bank before you sign.

A high-risk merchant account won't open card acceptance for THC

A high-risk merchant account is a card processing account for businesses that mainstream processors avoid, and it doesn't get around the network prohibition on cannabis purchases, whatever an offer of cannabis merchant services implies. PaymentCloud, which sells these accounts, defines them as specialized accounts for high-risk industries and says some providers require reserves that grow as your risk grows.

If you sell hemp or CBD, check this category carefully, because it's shifting. Stripe treats CBD with negligible THC as a regulated industry needing extra review, while it prohibits CBD over the local THC limit. The new federal hemp definition takes effect for most hemp products on 12/11/2026 under Public Law 119-103, signed 09/02/2026, and on 11/12/2026 only for a narrow group that law-firm summaries describe as synthetic cannabinoids, so if you sell hemp, expect your processor to look at your products again. Have your attorney confirm where each product lands.

Questions to ask cannabis payment processors

Get these answers in writing before you switch a register or sign a contract. A provider that's running clean can answer every one, and a vague answer tells you what you need to know.

  • Which network carries each transaction, and how is it coded on the shopper's statement?
  • Which bank or credit union sits behind this, and does that institution know it's cannabis?
  • What happens to held or settled funds if the program shuts down?
  • What are the full fees: per transaction, monthly, statement, platform and returned payment?
  • How fast does money reach my account, and who eats a returned payment?
  • What's my backup if this stops working on a Saturday night?

Paying between licensed businesses can run on ACH, checks and e-checks

Business-to-business cannabis payments can run on bank rails where a bank will process them, so a dispensary can pay a vendor without a card network involved, though some participants told GAO they must rely on cash for most business transactions. Those are the payments that strain a store most, because a wholesale invoice is far larger than a basket at the register. GAO reports that participants in seven focus groups said limited payment options hurt their ability to collect revenue from other businesses, and that the alternatives in use include checks, wire transfers and ACH.

  • Cannabis ACH. An electronic transfer from one bank account to another. It's fast and leaves a clean record, and the receiving business needs to give its account details.
  • Mailed check. Slower, but every vendor knows how to deposit one, and it works for a payee with no electronic setup.
  • E-check. A check delivered electronically that the vendor deposits. It carries the speed of email and the familiarity of a check.

You still need a bank account at an institution willing to take cannabis funds. Frequently asked questions

Can dispensaries take credit cards?

No, not for cannabis purchases. Mastercard and Visa prohibit using their cards to buy cannabis, and federal rescheduling for state medical licensees has not changed that. Be wary of any processor that says otherwise, because the usual method is disguising the sale as something else.

What is a cashless ATM?

It's an in-store device that runs a shopper's debit card and codes the purchase as an ATM withdrawal, in an amount that may be rounded up, with change handed back in cash. Visa objected that these purchases are miscoded as withdrawals, and Mastercard objected because cannabis sales are federally illegal. Some vendors still sell versions of it, so read your contract and keep a backup.

What is pay-by-bank at a dispensary?

The shopper links a checking account and the purchase is pulled over ACH instead of a card network. Several cannabis point-of-sale companies and payment apps offer it for in-store and online orders. Compare fees, settlement timing and who covers a returned payment.

What is a high-risk merchant account, and will one work for cannabis?

It's a card processing account for industries mainstream processors avoid, often with higher costs and reserves. It does not get around the network prohibition on cannabis purchases. If you sell hemp or CBD, have an attorney confirm each product before you apply.

Can cannabis businesses use ACH?

Yes, provided a bank or credit union will process it for you. Where a bank will process it, ACH lets licensed businesses pay each other without touching a card network. We send ACH, mailed checks and e-checks through our banking partners from one screen.

What's the difference between customer payments and paying between licensed businesses?

Customer checkout is a shopper paying a store, and card network rules shape every option there. Business-to-business payments are a store paying a vendor or another licensee, and those can run on bank rails such as ACH and checks where a bank will process them. We focus on the business-to-business side and build what's missing.
