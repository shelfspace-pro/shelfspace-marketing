The Cannabis Month-End Close Checklist

Six steps to close your dispensary's books clean every month — vendors reconciled, credits recovered, receivables worked, Metrc verified, QuickBooks in sync. The order to run them, and what breaks when you skip one.

At a glance

  • The cannabis month-end close is six repeatable steps: reconcile AP, close consignment settlements, generate credit memos, work AR, verify Metrc, sync QuickBooks
  • Run in order, the checklist produces clean books your CPA can file from — and catches the money vendors owe you before the period closes
  • Skip credit recovery and $8K–$25K a month in returns, expirations, and co-marketing dollars sits unclaimed
  • By hand it's 10–15 hours a week; ShelfSpace runs the whole checklist against the Metrc and POS data you already have
  • Delivered your way — software you approve in minutes a week, or fully done for you

Every dispensary hits the last week of the month with the same quiet dread: the close. The bank account gets balanced, a few checks get cut, and someone calls it done. But a real cannabis month-end close is not "did the bank balance." It's six specific jobs, run in a specific order, and each one catches money the others miss. Skip one and the leak is invisible until a vendor calls, an auditor asks, or your CPA sends back a question you can't answer.

This is the checklist — the dispensary back office checklist we run for operators every month. Six steps, what "good" looks like at each, and the exact failure mode if you skip it. Print it, tape it to the wall, or hand the whole thing to us. Either way, run all six in this order.

Why the order matters

The steps aren't interchangeable. You verify what physically arrived before you trust the invoices you're paying. You close settlements before you generate credits, because a credit is a line against a real sale. You work receivables while the aging is fresh, not after it hardens. And you sync to QuickBooks last, once the numbers are true — because a clean sync of dirty data just moves the mess into your general ledger faster. Here's the sequence.

1

Reconcile accounts payable

What good looks like: every vendor invoice for the period is three-way matched — invoice to purchase order to the actual delivery — with duplicates and overcharges flagged before a dollar goes out, then paid with a check the vendor can deposit. Skip it and: you pay the same invoice twice, or pay for a phantom line that never arrived, and you find out months later when the vendor's own books disagree with yours.

2

Close consignment settlements

What good looks like: for every vendor with product on your shelf, sell-through is reconciled against what they consigned, the agreed split is applied, and each vendor is settled to the penny with a report both sides trust. Skip it and: you're guessing at what you owe — overpaying vendors whose product moved slowly and underpaying the ones that sold out, which is how a good vendor relationship quietly turns into a dispute.

3

Generate credit memos

What good looks like: every return, expired unit, damaged item, and co-marketing dollar owed under the vendor agreement is captured on a credit memo and netted against what you pay that vendor. Skip it and: this is the single most expensive miss in the close — the money vendors owe you back that nobody tracks, which is why $8K to $25K a month goes unrecovered at operators who "already do their books."

4

Age and work accounts receivable

What good looks like: if you also wholesale, every open invoice is aged into buckets, reminders go out on the ones past terms, and the truly overdue accounts get escalated — before net-30 quietly becomes net-90. Skip it and: your receivables harden, your cash gets tied up in other operators' stores, and you end up handing accounts to a collection agency that takes 25% to 50% off the top.

5

Verify Metrc lines match what you received

What good looks like: every invoice line for the period is checked against the Metrc manifest and the physical receipt, so quantities, packages, and product all agree with the state's record of truth. Skip it and: you pay for units that never left the manifest, your inventory drifts from Metrc, and a discrepancy that would've taken a minute this month becomes an audit finding next quarter.

6

Sync everything to QuickBooks

What good looks like: every bill, payment, settlement, and credit for the period lands in QuickBooks already categorized the way your CPA needs it, so the close feeds your accountant a finished ledger. Skip it and: your books lag reality, your CPA rebuilds the month from receipts at their hourly rate, and you can't answer a 280E question because the data lives in four places.

That's the whole checklist. The steps are simple to state and brutal to do by hand, because cannabis makes every one of them harder than it would be in any other retail category.

Why the cannabis close is harder than retail

In a normal store, you match an invoice to a receipt and move on. In cannabis, the invoice has to agree with Metrc and the delivery, the card rails don't work so vendors get paid by Check 21 check, a big share of your product is on consignment so "what you owe" depends on what sold, and the credits vendors owe you back are buried in returns and expiration data nobody exports. Four of the six steps above simply don't exist for a coffee shop. That's why a general bookkeeper closes the bank account and calls it done — the cannabis-specific money never enters their view.

A close that only balances the bank account will pass every month while $8K–$25K in recoverable vendor credits quietly walks out the door.

What it costs to run it by hand

Add up the six steps for a single location and you land at 10 to 15 hours a week — invoice matching, settlement spreadsheets, credit tracking, collections email, Metrc cross-checks, and the reconciliation your bookkeeper needs. Multi-location groups don't add that cost, they multiply it: every new store is another vendor list, another settlement run, another aging report. Most operators cover it one of two ways, and both leak. They put it on an office manager who's already stretched, or they pay an outside bookkeeping firm a few thousand a month — and neither one is hunting for the credits a vendor owes back, because that's not what a general ledger is for.

Frequently asked questions

What is a cannabis month-end close checklist?

A cannabis month-end close checklist is the fixed set of money tasks a dispensary runs every month to close its books: reconcile accounts payable against Metrc and deliveries, close consignment settlements for the period, generate vendor credit memos for returns and expirations, age and work overdue accounts receivable, verify that Metrc lines match what you actually received, and sync everything to QuickBooks. Run in that order, it produces clean books your CPA can file from and catches the money vendors owe you before the period closes.

How long does a dispensary month-end close take by hand?

For most single-location dispensaries, the full month-end close runs 10 to 15 hours a week once you add up invoice matching, settlement spreadsheets, credit tracking, collections email, and the reconciliation your bookkeeper needs. Multi-location groups multiply that with every store. ShelfSpace runs the whole checklist against the Metrc and POS data you already have, so a close that took days becomes an approval that takes minutes.

What is the difference between closing AP and closing consignment settlements?

Accounts payable is what you owe for product you bought outright — you match the invoice to the delivery and pay it. Consignment settlements are for product a vendor put on your shelf that you only owe for once it sells — you reconcile sell-through against the manifest, apply the agreed split, and pay for exactly what rang up. Both close monthly, but they are different calculations and skipping the settlement step is how vendors get overpaid or underpaid without anyone noticing.

Do I still need my CPA if ShelfSpace runs the month-end close?

ShelfSpace runs the operational close — AP, settlements, credit recovery, and collections — and hands your accountant clean, categorized, QuickBooks-ready books. Every bill, payment, settlement, and credit is already coded when the CPA opens the ledger, so the close feeds your accountant instead of dumping a shoebox on their desk.

Where do most dispensary month-end closes go wrong?

The two most expensive misses are skipped credit recovery and unmatched Metrc lines. Credits for returns, expirations, and co-marketing go unclaimed because no one systematically tracks them — that is how $8K to $25K a month in recoverable dollars sits on the table at a single Massachusetts operator. Unmatched Metrc lines mean you paid an invoice for product that never physically arrived. Both survive a close that only balances the bank account.
