Cannabis Banking Guide

Cannabis Banking: How Cannabis Businesses Actually Bank Today

Cannabis businesses can bank, but only with institutions that choose to take on the compliance work. Here's how the system runs, what it costs, where federal law stands, and how we help you solve the problem.

Your bank just asked what your business actually does, or a banker told you they don't touch cannabis, or you're counting cash in a back room and wondering how anyone in this industry pays a vendor. Cannabis banking exists and it works, but it runs on a different set of rules from normal business banking. This guide explains how those rules work, why they make the process slow and expensive, and where federal law stands today.

How cannabis businesses bank today

Cannabis businesses bank through specific banks and credit unions that have chosen to serve them and have built a compliance program to do it. So yes, a cannabis company can have a bank account, and a licensed dispensary can too. The catch is that you can't assume any bank near you will open one, and the one that does will watch the account far more closely than it watches a hardware store's.

The pool is larger than most owners expect. FinCEN's public metrics spreadsheet, with data through 12/31/2024, counts 507 banks and 182 credit unions filing marijuana-related suspicious activity reports in the most recent quarter it shows. A filing count doesn't tell you who advertises or who is accepting new customers. Our cannabis bank directory lists institutions that say publicly, on their own websites, that they serve cannabis businesses.

What a cannabis bank is

A cannabis bank is an ordinary chartered bank or credit union with a dedicated program for cannabis customers. These institutions choose to serve the industry under FinCEN's guidance. What sets one apart is its people, its monitoring and its willingness to take the work on.

You'll run into a few structures:

  • Community banks and credit unions with an in-house team. Many serve one state, or only part of one, and a credit union also has to fit you within its membership rules.
  • Multi-state programs. KeyPoint Credit Union describes its cannabis program as nationwide, subject to its own compliance review, and Herring Bank lists markets across the country.
  • Banking programs run with partners. Safe Harbor says deposits sit directly with its federally insured bank and credit union partners. Ask exactly which institution holds your money.

Most programs also separate plant-touching businesses from companies that only serve them. Credit Union 1, for example, offers one checking account for licensed operators and another for ancillary businesses. Know which group you fall into before you apply, because the paperwork and the price can both change.

Why banks don't do business with dispensaries

Banks avoid dispensaries because marijuana activity is still illegal under federal law for most of the industry, and handling the proceeds can create federal exposure for the bank itself. That risk comes with a heavy compliance workload, so many institutions decide the customers aren't worth it.

The legal exposure is real. FinCEN's 2014 guidance opens by noting that the Controlled Substances Act makes it illegal under federal law to manufacture, distribute or dispense marijuana. The Attorney General's memo of 01/04/2018 added that marijuana activity may serve as the basis for prosecution under the money laundering statutes, the unlicensed money transmitter statute and the Bank Secrecy Act. That same memo rescinded the earlier Justice Department guidance on marijuana enforcement, including the Cole Memo that FinCEN's guidance is built around.

The workload is real too. First Enterprise Bank in Oklahoma explains its higher fees plainly on its own site: FinCEN and the FDIC watch cash transactions very closely, and the bank has a lot of back-office work to do to stay in compliance. That's the trade every institution weighs, and it's why TASI Bank notes on its cannabis page that many banks may choose not to serve these businesses.

What the FinCEN guidance requires

The FinCEN guidance is the working rulebook for cannabis banking. Issued on 02/14/2014 as FIN-2014-G001, it doesn't make marijuana legal or protect anyone from prosecution. It tells a financial institution what it's expected to do under the Bank Secrecy Act if it chooses to serve a marijuana-related business.

Before and during the relationship, the guidance expects the institution to:

  • Verify with state authorities that the business is licensed and registered.
  • Review the license application and related documents.
  • Request available information about the business and related parties from state licensing and enforcement agencies.
  • Understand the expected activity, including the products sold and the customers served.
  • Monitor public sources for bad news about the business and the people behind it.
  • Monitor the account for suspicious activity, including a published list of red flags.
  • Refresh all of that periodically, in proportion to the risk.

It also sets out three kinds of suspicious activity report: "Marijuana Limited" for a business that appears clean, "Marijuana Priority" for one that raises a concern, and "Marijuana Termination" when the institution ends the relationship. Read the point that surprises most owners twice: a bank serving a fully compliant dispensary still files reports on it. That's how the system is designed, and it doesn't mean you did anything wrong.

Why cannabis banking is slow

Cannabis banking is slow because every account requires an investigation before it opens and a repeating review after it opens. A normal business account asks for far less. A cannabis account needs license checks, owner checks and a picture of your expected cash before anyone can say yes.

Published timelines give a sense of the range. Safe Harbor says onboarding takes a few days to a few weeks depending on complexity, and that assumes a clean file. Multiple licenses, out-of-state owners, outside investors and vague entity names all add questions, and several of those appear on FinCEN's red flag list.

The work doesn't stop at opening. Honor Credit Union says its banker collects monthly documents and visits businesses on site, and Evergreen Credit Union says monthly and annual compliance requirements apply for the life of the relationship. FinCEN's own banking update refers to a 90-day timeframe for follow-on reports, so the review keeps coming back around.

Availability can also change. One Oklahoma bank's cannabis page now says it's no longer accepting new cannabis account openings, so confirm a program is open before you build a plan around it. Our guide to what happens when a cannabis bank shuts down covers how to keep payments moving if your provider changes.

What cannabis banking costs

Published monthly fees start in the hundreds of dollars, and percentage-based programs, such as Santa Cruz Community Credit Union's, can reach thousands. Prices vary widely, and many institutions don't publish theirs. These published examples show the range, and they aren't a ranking:

  • Dedham Savings in Massachusetts publishes a $250 one-time opening fee and a $400 monthly service fee on its Cannabis Checking account.
  • First Enterprise Bank in Oklahoma publishes a $400 monthly charge for growers, processors and dispensaries, and $200 for its ancillary tier.
  • Santa Cruz Community Credit Union's cannabis fee schedule, effective 02/01/2022, charges 0.4% of incoming funds in the first year and 0.3% after that, with a $750 monthly minimum for licensed businesses.

Here's a worked example, not customer data, using that Santa Cruz schedule: a licensed business with $200,000 of incoming funds in a month would pay $800 in its first year, and the $750 minimum after that, since 0.3% comes to $600. Count every charge before you compare, including armored pickup, wires and any annual review fee. Our guide to the cannabis business bank account walks through what to ask.

The SAFE Banking Act is not law

No, the SAFE Banking Act is not law. It has been introduced again in both chambers of Congress, and it's still sitting in committee.

The Senate version, S. 4942, was introduced on 06/24/2026 and referred to the Banking Committee. The House version, H.R. 9471, was introduced on 06/25/2026, and the latest action in the record we read is a referral to a subcommittee on 07/17/2026. Its House sponsor's release says the bill has passed the House seven times, and a prior Senate version, S. 2860, was reported out of the Senate Banking Committee on 09/28/2023 without becoming law.

If it passed, the Senate sponsor's summary says it would bar federal regulators from penalizing banks for serving state-legal cannabis businesses and create a safe harbor from prosecution and forfeiture for those banks, while keeping each bank's right to say no. The bill text also tells Treasury to amend the 2014 guidance or issue new guidance within 180 days of enactment. Banks would still have compliance work to do, so don't plan your business around this bill.

Where federal law stands on cannabis banking today

Federal law moved partway this year, and the banking rules haven't changed with it. A federal order effective 04/28/2026 moved marijuana under a state medical license, along with FDA-approved marijuana drug products, to Schedule III, while adult-use marijuana stayed in Schedule I. The text of that order doesn't mention banks or FinCEN.

The separate case on moving all marijuana to Schedule III is still open. A DEA judge stayed it on 09/29/2026 and, by an order dated 10/22/2026, kept it stayed only until briefing closes, with replies due 11/05/2026; no final rule has been issued. FinCEN's 2014 guidance remains on its website, and we found no replacement there. If you hold both medical and adult-use licenses, ask your bank directly how it treats each one, and don't assume rescheduling changed its answer.

What to do next if you need cannabis banking

Start with a short list of institutions in your state, a clean document file and a clear description of your business. That combination gets you real answers faster than cold calling every branch in town.

  • Find who's open. Use our directory by state, then check your state's resources. Washington's financial regulator, for one, publishes a list of providers that have told it they may serve the industry.
  • Build your file first. Licenses, formation papers, owner details and a monthly cash and deposit estimate cover most first requests.
  • Tell the truth about your business. Using a vague name such as "consulting" to hide marijuana activity is on FinCEN's red flag list, and a bank that finds out later can close the account.
  • Clean up your cash handling. Our guide to how dispensaries handle cash covers the basics, and breaking up deposits to avoid a report is a federal offense.
  • Line up a backup. Programs change, so keep a second institution in mind before you need it.

Frequently asked questions

How do cannabis businesses bank?

They use banks and credit unions that have chosen to serve cannabis and have built a compliance program for it. These institutions verify licenses, review owners, monitor account activity and file reports with FinCEN. Many also arrange armored cash pickup, ACH and wires.

Can a cannabis company have a bank account?

Yes. Licensed cannabis businesses and many of the companies that serve them can open accounts at institutions that accept them. Approval is never automatic, and each institution sets its own rules on license types, states and owners.

Why don't banks do business with dispensaries?

Most marijuana activity remains a federal crime, and handling the proceeds can create federal exposure for a bank. Serving a dispensary also requires extra due diligence, monitoring and reporting. Many banks decide that risk and workload aren't worth it, though plenty of smaller institutions have said yes.

What is a cannabis bank?

It's an ordinary chartered bank or credit union with a dedicated cannabis program. The difference is a compliance team that handles FinCEN's expectations and a willingness to take the account.

Is the SAFE Banking Act law?

No. The current bills have been introduced in the House and Senate but have not passed either chamber in this Congress. Banks still work under FinCEN's guidance, and any bank can still decline a cannabis customer.

Did rescheduling fix cannabis banking?

No. The federal order moved state-licensed medical marijuana to Schedule III, while adult-use marijuana stayed in Schedule I. The order doesn't address banking, a broader rescheduling case is still open with no final rule, and we found no replacement for FinCEN's guidance. Ask your own bank how it treats each license you hold.
