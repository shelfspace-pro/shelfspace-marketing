Accountable Plan Guide

Accountable Plan: The IRS Rules for Reimbursing Employees Without Adding to Their Wages

An accountable plan is a reimbursement arrangement that meets three IRS rules: a business reason for the expense, a timely accounting with receipts, and a return of anything paid in excess. Meet them and the reimbursement isn't wages. Miss them and it's taxed like a paycheck.

At a glance

  • Three rules make a plan accountable, per IRS Publication 463: a business connection, an adequate accounting within a reasonable period, and a return of any excess within a reasonable period.
  • Payments under an accountable plan "aren't wages," Publication 15 says, and payments under a nonaccountable plan are supplemental wages.
  • The IRS safe harbor in Publication 463: account for an expense within 60 days and return any excess within 120 days.
  • Receipts are the rule, and Publication 463 excuses an expense other than lodging that's less than $75.
  • Cannabis companies under 280E should set up the plan with their CPA, because 280E changes what the company itself can deduct.

Every company that pays employees back for gas, supplies or a trip has a reimbursement plan, written down or not. The only question is whether it's accountable. If it is, the money is a business expense repaid. If it isn't, the IRS treats it as pay, with withholding and payroll taxes on top.

This guide covers the three rules, the deadlines the IRS accepts, what counts as a receipt, and how to run a plan that holds up without chasing people for paper.

What an accountable plan is

An accountable plan is a reimbursement or allowance arrangement that includes all three of the IRS's rules. Publication 463 lists them:

  • Business connection. The employee paid or incurred deductible expenses "while performing services as an employee of your employer."
  • Adequate accounting. The employee accounts to the employer for the expenses within a reasonable period of time.
  • Return of excess. The employee returns any reimbursement or allowance above the expenses they accounted for, within a reasonable period of time.

The employer's version in Publication 15 adds one condition worth reading twice: the reimbursement "must not be an amount that would have otherwise been paid to the employee as wages." Swapping part of someone's salary for a tax-free allowance doesn't qualify.

What happens when reimbursements miss the rules

Reimbursements that miss the rules become wages. Publication 15 says payments under a nonaccountable plan "are wages and are treated as supplemental wages," subject to income, social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes. The same page says amounts paid under an accountable plan "aren't wages" and aren't subject to those taxes.

One plan can produce both. If an employee doesn't substantiate an expense or keeps an unused advance past the deadline, Publication 15 treats only the excess as paid under a nonaccountable plan, taxed in the first payroll period after the deadline passes.

Two traps make a whole arrangement nonaccountable. Publication 463 says an arrangement that repays expenses by reducing the employee's reported wages is a nonaccountable plan. And under Treasury regulation 1.62-2, an employee can't turn a nonaccountable plan into an accountable one by voluntarily handing in receipts after the fact. The plan has to require them.

The deadlines: 60 days to account, 120 days to return

A "reasonable period of time" depends on the facts, but Publication 463 lists actions the IRS treats as reasonable regardless of the facts:

  • The employee receives an advance within 30 days of the time they have the expense.
  • The employee adequately accounts for expenses within 60 days after they were paid or incurred.
  • The employee returns any excess reimbursement within 120 days after the expense was paid or incurred.
  • Or the employer sends a periodic statement, at least quarterly, asking the employee to return or account for outstanding advances, and the employee complies within 120 days of the statement.

Regulation 1.62-2 names these the fixed date method and the periodic payment method. Pick one with your CPA and write it into your policy, so nobody has to argue about what "reasonable" meant later.

What counts as a receipt

Receipts, canceled checks and bills are the evidence the IRS expects. Publication 463 says documentary evidence is ordinarily adequate if it shows the amount, date, place and essential character of the expense. It also lists when you don't need one:

  • An expense, other than lodging, that's less than $75, per Publication 463.
  • A transportation expense for which a receipt isn't readily available.
  • Meals or lodging while traveling, accounted for under a per diem allowance.

Skipping the receipt doesn't skip the record. The employee still has to account for the expense, so your policy should still ask what it was, when, where and why. Requiring a receipt for everything is simpler: a photo takes seconds, and nobody has to remember the exceptions.

Mileage works differently. Under an accountable plan, Publication 15's table treats amounts up to the standard mileage rate as exempt and the excess as taxable. The IRS set the 2026 business rate at 72.5 cents a mile, and the Journal of Accountancy reports it rose to 76 cents from 07/01/2026, so a trip's date decides its rate this year.

S corp owners and officers

An owner who works in an incorporated business is usually an employee too. Publication 15 says that if the business is incorporated, "corporate officers who work in the business are employees of the corporation," and its accountable plan section is written for employees. If that describes you, ask your CPA whether your own out-of-pocket costs can run through the company's plan. If they do, hold owners to the same receipts and deadlines as everyone else.

Cannabis companies: set it up with your CPA

A cannabis company needs the same plan, plus one more conversation. Section 280E limits what a business trafficking in Schedule I or II substances can deduct, which our 280E guide covers. Whether a reimbursed expense is wages to the employee and whether the company can deduct it are separate questions, so have your CPA set up the plan and say how each kind of expense should be coded.

Good records help either way. Code each expense to the right account, and if you run more than one license or entity, keep each expense with the entity that incurred it. That's the detail your CPA will ask for at tax time.

How to set up an accountable plan

  • Write the policy. Say what's reimbursable, what proof is required, and the deadlines. Our expense reimbursement policy template is a starting point for your counsel to review.
  • Set the deadlines. Use the 60 and 120 days from Publication 463, or the quarterly statement method, and apply them to everyone.
  • Require the evidence. A receipt or equivalent record for every expense, and no reimbursement without it.
  • Approve before you pay. Someone other than the employee checks the business reason and the coding.
  • Collect the excess. Track unused advances and get them back, or put them through payroll as wages.
  • Pay it as a reimbursement. Keep it separate from salary on the pay record, and keep the report and receipts with the payment. If you keep books in Xero, our guide to reimbursing employees in Xero covers how to record it.

Frequently asked questions

What is an accountable plan?

It's a reimbursement or allowance arrangement that meets three IRS rules: the expenses have a business connection, the employee adequately accounts for them within a reasonable period, and the employee returns any excess within a reasonable period. Reimbursements under it aren't wages.

What happens if reimbursements don't meet the accountable plan rules?

They're treated as paid under a nonaccountable plan. IRS Publication 15 says those payments are wages, treated as supplemental wages and subject to income, social security, Medicare and FUTA taxes.

How long do employees have to turn in receipts under an accountable plan?

The IRS treats an employee as acting within a reasonable period if they account for an expense within 60 days after paying or incurring it, and return any excess within 120 days. Other periods can qualify depending on the facts, so set your deadline with your CPA.

Do you need a receipt for every reimbursed expense?

Generally yes: the IRS asks for documentary evidence such as receipts, canceled checks or bills. Publication 463 lists exceptions, including an expense other than lodging that's less than $75 and a transportation expense with no receipt readily available. The under-$75 exception doesn't cover lodging.

Can an S corp owner be reimbursed under an accountable plan?

IRS Publication 15 says corporate officers who work in an incorporated business are employees of the corporation, and the accountable plan rules apply to employees. Have your CPA confirm how they apply to your ownership and role before you rely on them.

Is mileage reimbursement taxable?

Not under an accountable plan when it doesn't exceed the IRS standard mileage rate, according to the table in Publication 15. The part above the standard rate is taxable. The IRS business rate was 72.5 cents a mile for 2026 and rose to 76 cents from 07/01/2026.
