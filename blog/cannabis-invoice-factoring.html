Cannabis Invoice Factoring: How It Works and What It Really Costs

Factoring turns a slow store's invoice into cash this week, and you pay for it out of the invoice. Here's how the deal works, what it costs, and how to need less of it by collecting faster on what stores already owe you.

At a glance

  • Factoring is a sale, not a loan: you sell an unpaid invoice at a discount, get most of it now, and the rest, minus the fee, when the store pays.
  • Expect a lien filing, and expect your store to be told to pay the factor instead of you. Under UCC 9-406, once it gets that notice, paying you no longer clears its bill.
  • Recourse means you still carry the risk that the store never pays. Read that clause before anything else.
  • Cannabis factoring is harder to find and usually pricier, because federal rules make many banks and lenders cautious and the factor is underwriting your slowest stores.
  • When the fee is charged by the month, it grows the longer a store takes to pay, so the cheapest cash is the invoice you collect yourself, sooner.

You shipped the product, the store sold it, and the invoice is still open. Payroll is Friday, the next harvest needs inputs, and the money you already earned is sitting in a dispensary's bank account. That's usually when cannabis invoice factoring comes up: someone offers to hand you cash today for the invoices your stores haven't paid.

It can work. It also costs more than it looks, it changes who your stores talk to about money, and it doesn't fix the reason the invoices are slow. This guide walks through how factoring works, why it's harder and pricier in cannabis, a worked example of the real cost, and the cheaper move most brands should make first.

Not financial or legal advice. Talk to your attorney and accountant before you sign a factoring agreement. Sources checked 11/16/2026.

Factoring sells your invoices, it doesn't lend against them

Invoice factoring means selling your unpaid invoices to a company, called a factor, at a discount. The factor pays you part of the invoice now and the rest, minus its fee, after your store pays. You get cash without a loan payment, and you give up a slice of every invoice you sell.

The OCC's handbook on receivables financing describes it plainly: the factor buys the receivables at a discount, the customers "are notified to remit payments directly to the factor," and factors often take over collecting them. In factoring language, you are the "client" and your dispensary is the "customer."

The law treats it as a sale, too. UCC 9-109(a)(3) puts "a sale of accounts" under Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code, the same article that governs secured loans. That's why a factor files a financing statement, the form most people call a UCC-1, against your business, just as a lender would.

How cannabis invoice factoring works, step by step

A factoring deal runs in five steps: the factor reviews your stores, buys the invoices it accepts, pays you an advance, tells the store where to pay, and settles up when the money arrives. The details that decide your cost, like the advance, the reserve, the fee and recourse, are all set in your contract.

1

The factor checks your stores, not just you

It looks at each dispensary's payment record and sets a limit per store. Invoices from slow or risky stores can be turned down.

2

You sign, and it files a lien

The agreement covers the invoices you sell, and a financing statement goes on public record against your business.

3

You get an advance

Part of each invoice is paid to you up front. The rest is held back as a reserve until the store pays.

4

The store is told to pay the factor

A notice tells your dispensary the invoice was assigned and where payment now goes.

5

The store pays, and the reserve comes back, minus the fee

If the fee is charged by the month, a slower store means a bigger fee and less back to you.

The lien. Under UCC 9-310(a), outside a few listed exceptions, "a financing statement must be filed to perfect all security interests," and because Article 9 counts a buyer of accounts as holding a security interest, the factor files to protect its claim against your other creditors. Read what the filing covers. Ask the factor whether it covers only the invoices you sell or more of your business, and keep that in mind when you go to a lender later. Our guide to cannabis business loans covers what lenders look at before they say yes.

Recourse vs. non-recourse. This is the clause that decides who loses when a store never pays. With recourse, you do: the contract spells out how you cover the loss. With non-recourse, the factor carries the store's credit risk, within limits the contract spells out, so read exactly which losses it covers. The OCC describes classic factoring as "usually without recourse," and notes that banks buying receivables usually take them with recourse, which tells you the label varies by who's buying. Your contract is the only definition that counts.

Why factoring is harder and pricier in cannabis

Factoring costs more in cannabis because fewer funders will do it and the ones who do are taking on more risk. Federal rules make many banks and mainstream lenders cautious, courts haven't settled how cannabis contracts and bankruptcies are treated, and the factor's money rides on stores that already pay slowly.

  • Banks are cautious by design. FinCEN's guidance FIN-2014-G001 tells financial institutions serving marijuana businesses to verify state licenses, monitor the account and file suspicious activity reports, even when the business is legal in its state. Many stay out. Adult-use marijuana is still a Schedule I drug; only marijuana under a state medical license, and FDA-approved drug products containing it, moved to Schedule III, in a federal order effective 04/28/2026.
  • Most factors aren't banks anyway. The OCC notes that "most factoring is done by nonregulated businesses." In cannabis that means fewer bank options and a wide range of terms, so you have to read each contract closely.
  • The legal backstops are shaky. The NAIC's 2019 white paper on the cannabis market describes courts splitting on whether cannabis contracts can be enforced, and a federal appeals court upholding the dismissal of cannabis growers' bankruptcy case. A factor that can't count on those routes when a store fails prices that in.
  • The factor is underwriting your stores. The OCC says factors "focus most of their due diligence on the third-party customer," set limits for each customer based on payment status, and can reject invoices from individual customers. The stores you most want cash for are often the ones a factor wants least.

What cannabis invoice factoring really costs: a worked example

The real cost of factoring is the fee divided by the cash you actually got, over the days you had it. When the fee keeps running while a slow store sits on the invoice, a fee that sounds small per month can add up to a high annual rate. Here's the math with example numbers.

$16,000

advanced up front on an example $20,000 invoice

$1,600

example fee when the store pays at 60 days

About 61%

that example fee as a simple annual rate on the cash advanced

The example. These are example terms to show the math, not a market rate and not any factor's quote. Say you sell a $20,000 invoice to a store on net 30. The factor advances 80%, which is $16,000, and holds the other $4,000 as a reserve. Its fee is 4% of the invoice for every 30 days the invoice stays open.

The store pays at 60 days, so the fee is 8%, or $1,600. The factor releases the reserve minus the fee, which is $2,400. In total you receive $18,400 for a $20,000 invoice.

Now look at it as a rate. You paid $1,600 to use $16,000 for 60 days. That's 10% for two months, or about 61% a year as a simple annual rate. If the same store takes 90 days, the example fee rises to $2,400 and your reserve check shrinks to $1,600.

Across a book it adds up fast. At those same example terms, a brand that sells $100,000 of invoices a month to stores that pay at 60 days gives up $8,000 a month, or $96,000 a year, on money its stores already owed it.

Before you sign, turn any factoring quote into dollars per invoice and an annual rate on the cash you actually receive. Then add every other charge in the contract.

Real contracts carry more than one number. Ask for every charge in writing: the advance, the fee schedule and how it's counted, any minimum volume, and what happens at the end of the term. Then compare it with what a late invoice already costs you using our late invoice cost calculator.

What factoring does to the store relationship

Factoring usually puts a stranger between you and your store. The dispensary gets a notice to pay someone it has never dealt with, the factor may handle the follow-up, and if the factor won't buy a store's invoices, you're left deciding whether to keep selling to it on terms.

The notice is a legal switch. Under UCC 9-406(a), a store can pay you until it receives a proper notice that the invoice was assigned and "that payment is to be made to the assignee." After that, it "may not discharge the obligation by paying the assignor," which is you. A store that keeps paying you out of habit hasn't paid at all as far as the factor is concerned, and sorting that out lands on your desk.

Someone else's tone, your name. The OCC notes factors often run the collection work themselves. Your buyer may get reminders from a company with its own style and its own priorities. You worked hard for that shelf space, and you can't control how someone else asks a stressed store owner for money.

Rejections change who you sell to. When a factor turns down a store's invoices, the OCC says the seller "is likely to reduce or halt sales to that customer." In cannabis, that's often a store you'd rather keep and help through a slow month.

And the slow payer is still slow. Factoring moves the wait onto the factor's books and charges you for it. If a store is drifting toward not paying at all, read our warning signs a dispensary is about to stop paying before you sell its invoices or ship it more.

Who should still consider factoring

Factoring can make sense for a brand that has strong-paying stores, a short and specific cash gap, and a written quote it has turned into an annual rate. It makes less sense as a standing fix for stores that pay late, because you pay for that lateness on every invoice.

If you're weighing it, do the two things that cost nothing first. Find out how much of your open balance is simply past due and collectable, and put a real follow-up process on it. Frequently asked questions

How does cannabis invoice factoring work?

A cannabis brand sells its unpaid dispensary invoices to a factor at a discount. The factor pays an advance up front, holds back a reserve, tells the store to pay the factor instead of the brand, and returns the reserve minus its fee once the store pays. The factor usually files a lien on the brand's receivables.

What is the difference between recourse and non-recourse factoring?

With recourse factoring, the brand carries the risk that a store never pays, and the contract says how the brand covers the loss. With non-recourse factoring, the factor carries the store's credit risk, within limits the contract spells out. The contract defines which one you have.

Will my dispensary customers know I factored their invoices?

Usually, yes. Under UCC 9-406, a store can keep paying the brand until it receives notice that the invoice was assigned and that payment goes to the factor. After that notice, paying the brand no longer clears the store's debt, so factors typically send it.

How much does cannabis invoice factoring cost?

It depends on the factor, your stores' payment records and how long they take to pay, and on how the fee is counted. A fee charged for each month an invoice stays open grows when a store pays late. Get every charge in writing, work out the dollars per invoice, and divide the fee by the cash you actually receive over the days you hold it to see the annual rate.
