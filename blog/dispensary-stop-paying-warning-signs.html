Cannabis Credit Risk

Warning Signs a Dispensary Is About to Stop Paying

Many stores don't go dark overnight. The slide shows up in your invoices, your reps' store visits and outside records, usually while there's still time to cut your exposure.

At a glance

  • No single sign proves a store is failing. Two or three together, on one account, are your cue to act.
  • The strongest signs show up in your own books: payments that change shape, promises that slip, and new disputes on old invoices.
  • Your reps see the rest first: thin shelves, staff turnover and a buyer who suddenly can't be found.
  • Outside records add a check: license status, court filings and, for New York suppliers, the C.O.D. list in Metrc.
  • When the signs stack up, shrink your exposure in order: hold new credit, get a dated commitment, then secure what's owed.

Many stores that stop paying don't do it all at once. First the check that used to come on the 28th comes on the 40th. Then a full payment turns into a partial one. The buyer who answered every text starts answering every third. By the time the store is formally in trouble, you may have shipped it two more orders on credit.

Late payment alone won't tell you much, because it's common across the industry. The Cannabiz Credit Association has reported that more than 56% of cannabis invoices nationwide are past due. A late store is usually just a late store. What you're looking for is change: an account that's getting worse in ways that a slow bookkeeper can't explain.

The warning signs a dispensary is about to stop paying

The clearest warning signs are changes in behavior on one account: shrinking or irregular payments, broken promises, new disputes on old invoices, a buyer who goes quiet, and thinner shelves. None of these proves anything alone. When two or three appear together on one store, treat that account as a credit risk.

These signs are a reason to look closer, not proof. Treat them as prompts for a conversation and keep any accusations out of your messages.

Signs in your own invoices and payments

Your own ledger gives the earliest and most reliable warning, because it shows what the store actually does with its cash. Compare each account against its own history instead of against other stores. A store that always pays slowly may be fine, while a prompt payer that starts slipping deserves a call this week.

  • Days to pay are creeping up. Look for a steady climb over several invoices rather than one late bill. A store's own pattern is the baseline.
  • Payments change shape. Round-number payments on account, instead of payments against specific invoices, often mean the store is paying what it has rather than what it owes.
  • They pay the newest invoice and skip the oldest. That can be a way to keep product flowing while an old balance ages.
  • Payments fail. A returned check or a failed bank payment can be a clerical error once. A repeat is a cash problem until shown otherwise.
  • Disputes appear on old invoices. A real question usually surfaces soon after delivery. A new objection to an invoice that has sat for weeks can be a way to buy time. Check it against the Metrc manifest, answer it fast, and ask when the undisputed part will be paid.
  • Promises slip. One missed date happens. Two missed dates in a row on the same balance is a pattern, which is why it pays to write down every promise to pay.

Track the two numbers that show this at the account level: how long each store takes to pay, and how much of its balance is past due. Our DSO calculator gives you the measure, and our Fall 2026 payment data shows how slow payment looks across a real set of brand books.

Signs your reps can see at the store

Your reps often spot trouble before your ledger does, because a store short on cash shows it on the floor. Thin shelves, missing brands, unfamiliar faces and vague answers from the buyer are all worth reporting. Give reps one simple place to log these notes, so that a passing remark reaches whoever makes credit decisions.

  • Thin or unbalanced shelves. A store that can't get credit from its other suppliers often runs low across categories, or fills up with whatever a supplier will still ship.
  • Reorders shrink or stop while the balance stays open. The store may be buying elsewhere for cash, or winding down.
  • Sudden demand for big discounts or consignment. Asking for better terms is normal. A sudden push from a store that already owes you can mean it needs your product to fund other bills.
  • Key people leave. When the buyer, the bookkeeper or the general manager goes, you may lose both your contact and the person who knew your balance.
  • The buyer goes quiet. Calls move to voicemail, and messages get answered by someone new with no authority. Silence from a contact who used to respond is one of the strongest signals there is.
  • Talk of a sale. A sale can be good news, but it also means your balance needs a clear answer before ownership changes.

A rep who has to chase money can feel like they're risking the relationship. Our rep tools keep that ask simple, and the notes they bring back are often worth as much as the payment they collect.

Signs in outside records

Outside records can confirm what your ledger and reps are suggesting, and they catch problems a store won't mention. Check the regulator's license status, the state entity record and the local court records for any account that worries you. These checks cost little and can reveal a lawsuit or a lost license early.

  • License status. Look up the store with your state regulator. A suspended, surrendered or expiring license changes the risk immediately.
  • Entity status. The state's business entity record shows whether the company is still active. What else it lists varies by state.
  • Court filings. Search the court records for the store's county, where they're available, for suits by other suppliers, landlords or lenders. Another supplier's suit is a strong sign that you aren't alone.
  • State payment lists. In New York, the Office of Cannabis Management's delinquent payment guidance requires retailers buying on credit to pay within 30 days, and suppliers report those who don't. A reported store goes on the C.O.D. list, which suppliers can see in their Metrc accounts, so check it before every credit decision if you sell there. Other states have different rules, as our state-by-state guide shows.
  • Lien filings. A UCC search at the state filing office can show whether a lender or another supplier has already filed on the store's assets.

Don't stop at the store's name. Stores often operate under a trade name that differs from the legal entity on the license. Your credit application should give you the legal name, and that's the one to search.

What to do when the signs stack up

When two or three signs appear on one account, reduce your exposure in order: pause new credit, talk to the person who pays the bills, get a dated commitment, then put it in writing. Move quickly but stay polite. Plenty of struggling stores recover, and the ones that do remember who treated them fairly.

1

Hold new credit

Ship only for payment at delivery, or against a paid-down balance, until you have answers.

2

Talk to the decision maker

Ask the owner or whoever pays the bills what's going on, and listen for a real plan.

3

Get a date in writing

A specific amount, date and payment method, followed by a written confirmation.

4

Put it on a plan

If cash is the real problem, a signed plan gives you a schedule and a record.

5

Secure what you can

Ask your attorney about a guaranty or security interest before you extend anything further.

6

Send a final notice

When dates keep slipping, a firm pay-by date on your letterhead ends the back and forth.

The paperwork for each step already exists. Use a promise-to-pay letter to confirm a date, a payment plan agreement to set a schedule, and a personal guaranty if an owner agrees to stand behind the balance. Each template says the same thing we'll say here: this is not legal advice, so have your counsel review it before you use it. When dates keep slipping, send a final notice letter.

Before you pause credit in a state with its own payment rules, check what those rules require of you. New York, for one, sets its own process and timing for reporting a delinquent retailer.

The cost of reading the signs too late

Reading the signs late costs you twice: the balance that goes bad, and the orders you kept shipping while it did. Every delivery made on credit after the first warning adds to what you could lose. Holding one order can feel harsh, but it's cheaper than writing that order off later.

There's a timing reason to move early as well. A store with some cash left can still choose who gets paid, and it tends to pay the suppliers who ask clearly and often. Catching a slide takes a routine more than instinct: one weekly review of every past-due account, with payment history, promises and rep notes in the same place. Frequently asked questions

How can I tell if a dispensary is about to stop paying?

Watch for change on one account: days to pay climbing, partial or irregular payments, failed payments, missed promises, new disputes on old invoices, a buyer who goes quiet and thinning shelves. One sign alone proves little. Two or three together are your cue to pause new credit and talk to whoever pays the bills.

Should I stop shipping to a dispensary that pays late?

Not automatically, because late payment is common and many late stores do pay. Compare the account with its own history and look for other warning signs. If several appear, or the balance passes the limit in your credit policy, hold new credit or ship only for payment at delivery until you have a dated commitment.

Where can I check a dispensary's license or payment status?

Start with your state regulator's license lookup, the state's business entity record and the local court records. If you're a licensed supplier in New York, also check the C.O.D. list in your Metrc account, which names retailers that suppliers have reported as delinquent. Search the store's legal entity name, not just its trade name.

What should I do first when I see the warning signs?

Hold new credit, then talk to the owner or whoever pays the bills. Ask for a specific amount, date and payment method, and confirm it in writing. If cash is the real problem, offer a signed payment plan and ask your attorney about a personal guaranty or security interest before extending anything further.
